Expected or observed?

AC9M6P02 50–60 minutes Term 3, Week 9 Answers the open question
Shows teaching notes, misconceptions and answers
Students: use the tabs to move through the lesson. Everything you need to work on is on the Explore tab.

Learning intention

We are learning to work out how many times we expect an outcome, and to compare that with what actually happened.

Success criteria

  • I can calculate an expected frequency from a probability and a number of trials.
  • I can record what actually happened accurately.
  • I can find the difference between expected and observed.
  • I can explain why the two are almost never equal, and why that is normal.

Curriculum

AC9M6P02 — conduct repeated chance experiments and run simulations with an increasing number of trials using digital tools; compare observations with expected results and discuss the effect on variation of increasing the number of trials.

Take the butcher's paper down from the wall. The question students wrote in Lesson 2 — why doesn't the spinning match the calculation? — is what today begins to answer. Start the lesson by pointing at it. Two lessons of waiting is a long time in Year 6, and the payoff should be visible.
What today does and does not do. Today gives students the language and the calculation: expected frequency, observed frequency, and the gap between them. It establishes that the gap is normal.

It does not explain why more trials help. That is Lesson 5. Resist finishing the story today — the pooling activity in the Summarise points at Lesson 5 without resolving it.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs. The assessment has questions at all three levels.

SF — simple familiar

You have seen this kind of question before and it is clear what to do. One step, friendly numbers.

CF — complex familiar

You know the method, but there is more than one step, or you have to pick which method to use.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the method. You work out what to do, and explain why you did it.

A CU question is not a harder sum. It is usually one where you have to decide something and then justify it. Getting a number is not enough.

Warm-up · 7 minutes

Last one standing

Everyone stands. You are going to toss a coin twice. Before each round, students commit to a prediction using their hands:

  • Both hands on head — I predict two heads
  • Both hands on hips — I predict two tails
  • One hand on head, one on hip — I predict one of each

Toss twice. Anyone who predicted wrong sits down. Play until one student is left, then play again.

The warm-up is not filler — it is the lesson in miniature. Two coin tosses give four equally likely outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT. But grouped the way students are predicting, they are 1/4, 1/2, 1/4 — one of each is twice as likely as either double.

Most of the room will not know this at the start. After two or three rounds, ask who is still standing and what they chose. The survivors skew heavily towards "one of each", and students notice.

Do not explain it yet. Ask the question — "is one of these three choices better than the others?" — and take a vote. Then move to the Launch, where the bag has exactly the same structure: 1/4, 1/2, 1/4. Students who spotted the coin pattern will recognise the bag, and that recognition is worth more than being told.
If the room is large. Play in table groups with one coin each rather than one coin at the front, so nobody stands for four minutes without a turn. The pattern shows up faster with more rounds.

Launch · 10 minutes

The bag of four

Hold up an opaque bag. Show the class the contents, then close it: 2 red, 1 blue, 1 green.

Write the probability of each colour three ways.

Thirty seconds on whiteboards. Red 2/4 = 0.5 = 50%. Blue and green 1/4 = 0.25 = 25% each. Then the real question:

If I draw a counter 20 times, putting it back each time, how many reds should I get?

Take answers. Somebody will say 10. Ask how they know. Then formalise it:

expected frequency = probability × number of trials How many times the maths says it should happen

Both methods, on the board

Method 1 — divide, then multiplyMethod 2 — decimal first
There are 4 counters, so split the 20 draws into 4 equal parts.
20 ÷ 4 = 5.
Red is 2 of the 4 counters, so 5 × 2 = 10.		 Red is 2 out of 4, which is 0.5.
0.5 × 20 = 10.

Both methods are on the conversion mat. Students use whichever they prefer, and a calculator is fine for the dividing.

The word to be careful with. "Expected" does not mean "will get". Say this explicitly, today, before anyone runs a trial: expected frequency is what the maths predicts, and the real result will land somewhere near it. Students who miss this will read every result as an error.
Draw once, in front of them. Before releasing them to the Explore, draw a single counter from the bag and show it. If it is blue, say: "The expected number of reds in one draw is 0.5. I got zero reds. Is the maths wrong?" Ten seconds, and it does more work than a paragraph of explanation.

Explore · 24 minutes

Predict, then find out

Pairs build their own bag with the same composition: 2 red, 1 blue, 1 green. Then:

  1. Fill in the probability column for all three colours, three ways.
  2. Calculate the expected frequency for 40 trials, before drawing anything.
  3. Run 40 trials. Draw, record, put it back. Every time.
  4. Fill in observed, then difference. Difference = observed − expected, so it can be negative.
Order matters. The expected column is filled in before any drawing. If students run the trials first, they will fit their expected numbers to what they got, and the lesson is gone.

One set of results, invented. The differences add to zero — worth pointing at if a pair notices.

Two things to check while circulating.

1. Replacement. The counter goes back every time. Pairs that deal out four counters and stop have not run 40 trials.

2. The totals row. Observed must total 40 because they did 40 draws. If it does not, the recording went wrong and the pair should say so rather than adjust it. Recording an inconvenient result honestly is a habit this cycle is trying to build, and it is assessed in Part B.

The differences always sum to zero. Both columns total 40, so the differences cancel. A pair who spots this has found something real. Ask them whether it would still be true for any bag.

Part C · Show your thinking

1SF

A bag has 8 counters. 3 are red. You draw 40 times. How many reds do you expect? Show your working.

2CF

You expect 15 blues out of 60 draws. What is the probability of drawing a blue? Explain how you worked it out.

3CF

Look at the DIFFERENCE column in Part A. Add the three numbers together. What do you get? Why must that always happen?

1 15. Method 1: 40 ÷ 8 = 5, then 5 × 3 = 15. Method 2: 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375, then 0.375 × 40 = 15.
2 1/4. 15 out of 60 is 15/60, which simplifies to 1/4. Working backwards from the expected frequency.
3 Zero. Both the expected column and the observed column total 40, because you did 40 draws, so the differences cancel out exactly. A pair who spots this has found something real — ask whether it would be true for any bag.

Talk it through with a partner

Show your partner your answer to question 3. Your partner picks one of these to ask you:

  • Would the differences still add to zero if we did 100 draws?
  • Could one difference be bigger than all the others put together?
  • What would it mean if your differences did NOT add to zero?

Write down the question you were asked and what you said back.

★1 Red 40 ÷ 7 × 1 = 5.71, blue 11.43, green 22.86.
★2 It is an average, not a count. Over many sets of 40 draws you would average about 5.71 reds per set. In any single set you get a whole number.

Summarise · 10 minutes

Pool the whole class

Each pair adds their red count and their trial count to the class pooling chart. Total both columns.

Ask the right question — this is the one place today where it is easy to teach the opposite of the truth.
Do not ask "did the difference get smaller?"

It almost certainly got bigger. For one pair at 40 trials, the gap between observed and expected reds averages about 2.5. Pool thirteen pairs into 520 trials and the average gap grows to about 9.

What shrinks is the gap as a fraction. One pair is out by about 0.06 of their trials; the pooled class is out by about 0.02. That is the thing that improves, and it is the whole basis of Lesson 5.

So ask this instead: "Write the pooled reds as a fraction of all the trials. How close is that to one half?" Then have one pair write their own result as a fraction of 40 next to it. The class fraction will be visibly closer to 0.5 than almost any individual pair's.

If a student does raise the raw difference getting bigger, that is a good observation, not a wrong one. Say so, and say that Lesson 5 will sort out which number to watch.

Three things to land

  1. Expected frequency is calculated. Observed frequency is counted. One comes from the maths, the other from the world.
  2. They are almost never equal, and that is not a mistake. Nobody did anything wrong.
  3. The bigger the number of trials, the closer the fraction gets to the probability. Point at the pooled chart. Then stop — why this happens is Lesson 5.
Go back to the question we wrote in Lesson 2. Can anybody answer it now? Is there any part of it we still cannot answer?

Vocabulary

Add to the Frayer boards: trial, frequency, expected, observed. Four words is a lot for one lesson — do expected and observed together as a class and leave the other two for students to complete.

Review · 4 minutes

Exit ticket

A spinner has 5 equal sections. 2 of them are shaded. You spin it 30 times.
1SF

How many times do you expect it to land on shaded? Show your working.

2CF

Sam spun it 30 times and it landed on shaded 9 times. Did Sam make a mistake? Explain.

3CU

Sam says "the probability of shaded is 9 out of 30". Is Sam right? Explain your answer.

Answers.
1. 12. Method 1: 30 ÷ 5 = 6, then 6 × 2 = 12. Method 2: 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4, then 0.4 × 30 = 12. Accept either, but the working must be there — an unsupported 12 does not show which method the student holds.
2. No. 9 is not 12, but observed results are almost never exactly equal to expected. Nothing went wrong.
3. No. 9 out of 30 is what Sam observed. The probability is 2/5, which comes from the spinner itself and does not change because of what happened when Sam spun it.

Question 3 is the discriminator, and it matters more than it looks. A student who agrees with Sam has swapped observed frequency for probability. That confusion, left alone, makes Lesson 6 impossible — the whole "is this die fair?" task depends on knowing that a result is evidence about a probability rather than the probability itself. Anyone who agrees with Sam should be seated where you can reach them in Lesson 5.

Lesson-specific adjustments

Standing adjustments are on the linked Personalised Adjustments document and are not repeated here.

  • Trial count reduced to 20 where 40 is not sustainable. The pooled class chart still supplies the large-sample picture, so nothing is lost from the Summarise.
  • Recording table pre-formatted with the probability column already completed, so the cognitive load sits on the expected-frequency calculation rather than on yesterday's conversions.
  • Short trial blocks. Ten draws, mark the sheet, ten more. Forty consecutive draws is a long time to sustain attention on a repetitive task.
  • Named roles before drawing starts — one student draws and replaces, the other records, then swap at the halfway mark.
  • The warm-up is a standing game. Students who prefer not to stand can play seated with hands on the desk; the prediction signal works the same way.

Misconceptions to expect today

"The observed result IS the probability"

A pair draws 23 reds out of 40 and writes the probability as 23/40. This is the most important misconception in the whole cycle and today is where it becomes visible.

Response. "What was the probability before you started drawing? Did the counters change?" The bag did not change, so the probability did not change.

"I got 23 instead of 20, so I did it wrong"

Students treat the gap as an error and want to re-run until they get exactly 20. Some will quietly adjust their tally.

Response. Ask the room for the biggest gap anyone got, and celebrate it. Then ask whether anyone got exactly the expected number — usually one or two pairs, out of luck rather than skill.

"Expected means it will happen"

The everyday meaning of "expect" overrides the mathematical one. Students predict that the result will be exactly the expected frequency and are surprised every time.

Response. The everyday-versus-mathematical chart. "I expect my friend at four o'clock" does not mean they arrive at four o'clock exactly.

Drawing without replacement

Carried over from Lesson 3 and still the most common procedural error. With only four counters it wrecks the results fast.

Response. Catch it in the first two minutes of the Explore. After that, the pair has 40 trials of unusable data.
