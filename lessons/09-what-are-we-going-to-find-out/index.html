Lesson 9 · What are we going to find out?

The first lesson of Term 4 and the bridge between the two halves of the unit. Students sit the statistics diagnostic, meet the four data types, and design the sheet their team will actually carry at Buddy Games Day. Everything from Lesson 10 onwards uses the data this lesson decides to collect.

Games Day happens between this lesson and Lesson 10. The sheet agreed today is printed and carried on the day. If the collection goes wrong there is no second chance, which is the whole reason this lesson exists rather than starting Term 4 with graphs.

The honest shape of the design task

Teams genuinely design the instrument, and the Summarise converges the class on one agreed sheet — because twenty-eight different sheets cannot be pooled, compared in Lesson 12, or marked in Part B. The printed sheet on the Resources tab is that convergence point, with one column left blank for the class to name.

Say this to students rather than pretending otherwise. “We are all going to record the same four things so that we can compare teams later” is a real statistical reason, and it is the reason professional surveys use a common instrument. What the teams decide is the detail: the order of the columns, how the enjoyment scale is worded, and what goes in the spare column.

Reading the diagnostic tonight

The grouping comes from items 4, 5 and 6, not from a total. Item 7 — the truncated axis — identifies the two or three students who lead the critique groups in Lesson 14. Item 3 is today’s data types in disguise: students who called height and time categories are reading the label, not the value.

What we are learning today

We are learning to plan a statistical investigation: to name the kind of data we are collecting, and to design something we can actually fill in while a five-year-old is standing in front of us.

I will know I can do it when…

  • I can name the four types of data and give an example of each from our own Games Day;
  • I can turn a question that data cannot answer into one that it can;
  • I can design a recording sheet that someone else could use without asking me what it means;
  • I can say what my team will do on the day so that every row gets filled in.

Set your goal for today

Read the success criteria above. Which one is the biggest stretch for you? Write one thing you are going to focus on. You will come back to it at the end.

Not sure? The third one is where most teams come unstuck — a sheet that makes sense to you and to nobody else.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs.

SF — simple familiar

One step, and it is clear what to do. Naming the type of a variable sits here.

CF — complex familiar

More than one step, or you have to choose the method. Rewriting a question sits here.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the answer. Designing the jobs for the day sits here.

Today the hardest questions are not about numbers at all. They are about whether a plan survives contact with a room full of Preps.

Item 7 is not a fair test, and that is the point

Two column graphs show the same two numbers, one with the vertical axis starting at 17. Most students will not notice, because nobody has taught them to. A wrong answer here is not a gap to fill in Lesson 10.

What it identifies is the handful of students who already have the instinct. Those students lead the critique groups in Lesson 14, where the misleading newsletter is the whole lesson.

Do not total the scores

The grouping comes from items 4, 5 and 6. Items 4 or 5 wrong means the pre-built workbook in Lesson 10 and supplied intervals in Lesson 13. Items 4 to 6 right with item 7 missed is most of the class and is exactly where the unit expects them to be.

One shot at the data

“Games Day is on. Your game is built, you know its probabilities, and the Preps are coming.

Here is the part nobody thinks about until it is too late. Whatever you write down on the day is all the data you will ever have. You cannot go back on Monday and ask a Prep how much they liked it, because they will not remember, and because they are five.

So today we are not making graphs. Today we are deciding what to write down, and designing something you can fill in with one hand while a five-year-old pulls on your sleeve with the other.”

The four things we are going to record are on the Summarise tab. Do not show them yet — teams design first and then meet the agreed version, so the agreement means something.

Why this lesson is not optional

AC9M6ST03 is “plan and conduct statistical investigations”, and planning is the half that usually gets skipped because it produces no graph at the end. It is also the half that Part B item B2 assesses directly: describe how the data was collected, and identify one limitation of your method. A student who was handed a sheet cannot answer B2, because they made no decisions to describe.

If a team is away on Games Day

Pair them with another team now, today, with both names on both sheets. Lesson 12 asks every student to compare two distributions, and a student with no data of their own has nothing to compare. Fixing it today costs a minute; fixing it in Lesson 12 costs the lesson.

The four types

Two families, two each. A categorical variable puts each one into a group; a numerical variable gives each one a number.

Nominal — groups with no order

Red, blue, gold. Walk, car, bus. You could write them in any order and nothing would be wrong.

Ordinal — groups in an order

Did not enjoy, okay, liked it, loved it. The order matters, but the gaps between them are not measured.

Discrete and continuous — numbers

Discrete is counted and jumps in whole steps. Continuous is measured and can land anywhere in between.

The test that separates discrete from continuous: does half of one make sense? Half a win, no. Half a second, yes.

Questions

1SF

Name the type of each of these: (a) which outcome the buddy got on their first turn; (b) how much they liked the game, from “did not enjoy” to “loved it”; (c) how many of their three turns they won; (d) how long their whole visit took, in seconds.

2SF

One of those four needs a stopwatch and one needs nothing but your eyes. Which is which? Why does that matter when you are deciding who does what on the day?

3CF

“Was our game fun?” cannot be answered with data. Rewrite it as a question that can be. Then say what you would have to collect to answer it.

4CF

A team decides to record enjoyment as just “good” or “bad”. Compared with a four-point scale, name one thing their data will not be able to show.

5CF

Name one thing that will be hard to record while a five-year-old is standing in front of you. What will your team do about it?

6CU

Your team has three people and about twelve buddies will visit. Give each person a job so that every row of the sheet gets filled in. Say what happens first, second and third for one buddy.

7CU

Your sheet has one spare column. What is the one extra thing worth recording, and which of the four types is it? Justify it by what you will want to say about your game afterwards.

1. (a) nominal categorical — names with no order; (b) ordinal categorical — categories that go in an order; (c) discrete numerical — counted, and only whole numbers are possible; (d) continuous numerical — measured, and any value in between is possible. The giveaway for (c) against (d) is whether half of one makes sense. Half a win does not. Half a second does.

2. Time needs the stopwatch; the outcome needs only watching. This decides the jobs: the person holding the stopwatch cannot also be the one running the game, because they have to start and stop on the same event every time. Teams that do not sort this out get times that mean different things from row to row.

3. Any rewrite that names a group, a variable and a way of measuring it. “How much did the Prep buddies who played our game enjoy it?” works. “Did they like it?” still does not, because it has a yes or no answer for one person rather than a distribution for a group.

4. The strongest answer: with two categories you cannot see the shape — whether most buddies clustered at the top or spread out — and you cannot tell a buddy who merely liked it from one who loved it. Watch for students who say “it is less accurate”; press them for what specifically is lost.

5. Expect: the buddy wanders off before you record; two buddies arrive at once; you forget whose turn the time belongs to. The useful half of this question is the second half. “Write it straight away” is a plan. “Try to remember” is not.

6. A workable split is runner, recorder, timer, with the recorder filling the row before the next buddy starts. Judge the order, not the roles: a team that records everything at the end of the session has designed a memory test.

7. No set answer. Strong responses pick something they can use later — whether the buddy had played another team’s game first, say, which is nominal and would explain a slow time. Weak responses pick the buddy’s name, which is an identifier rather than something to analyse.

Now design your sheet

Start from the example row. Your planning sheet already has one buddy filled in. Copy its shape for the rest.
ASF

Which of these is a measurement, not a count? Number of turns won · Seconds taken · Outcome of the first turn

BSF

Put these four in order from “did not enjoy” to “loved it”, then give each one a number from 1 to 4.

CSF

Look at the example row on your sheet. Write the next row as if a buddy won two turns out of three, took 40 seconds, and loved it.

Once you have those three, write the jobs for your team as a list: who does what, in order.

Design it from blank. On your planning sheet, write a column heading for each of the four things, in the order you would fill them in on the day. Then write one example row as if a buddy had just played. Then write your team’s jobs.

Test it before you finish: hand your sheet to another team and ask them to fill in one row without you explaining anything. If they cannot, the headings are not clear enough yet.

These two are the ideas Part B asks about. Item B2 wants a limitation of your method, and this is where it comes from.
★1CU

Two teams record enjoyment on different scales — one uses four levels, the other uses three. What can still be compared between them, and what cannot?

★2CU

One buddy comes back and plays your game a second time. Does their second visit get a new row? Explain what each choice does to your results.

One sheet for the whole class

“Every team has designed a sheet, and they are all different. Here is the problem with that. In three lessons’ time I am going to ask you to compare your results with another team’s. If they recorded different things in a different way, there is nothing to compare.

So we are going to agree on four things that everybody records, in the same way. That is not me taking over — it is what any real survey does, and it is the only reason comparing teams will be possible at all. The fifth column is yours.”

What we recordTypeHow
Outcome of the buddy’s first turnNominal categoricalWrite the outcome name from your own game. First turn only.
Turns won out of threeDiscrete numerical0, 1, 2 or 3. Every buddy gets three turns.
Seconds for the whole visitContinuous numericalStopwatch. Start when they step up, stop when they walk away.
How much they liked itOrdinal categoricalCircle 1 to 4. Ask the buddy, do not guess for them.
The spare columnYour team decidesHead it yourselves. Write what type it is at the top.
First turn only for the outcome. That is a real decision and it has a reason: one value per buddy keeps the row simple, and it means the outcomes can be compared with the probabilities you calculated in Part A. Say this out loud — it is the link between the two halves of the unit.

Hold the line on the enjoyment scale

Teams will want to change the wording. Let them argue it for two minutes and then settle on one wording for the class, because an ordinal scale with different wording between teams is not comparable and Lesson 12 will stall. The four levels matter more than the words: three collapses the shape, five invites everyone to pick the middle.

What to do with the designs the class did not adopt

Keep them. A student who designed a six-point scale and had to give it up has a ready-made answer for Part B item B2 — a real limitation of the method, met first-hand rather than invented afterwards. Say so now, so they remember it in three weeks.

Exit ticket

On the back of your planning sheet. On your own, five minutes.
1SF

Name the type of each: the colour of a buddy’s sticker; the number of stickers left in the jar; how many minutes the queue was.

2CF

Your team wrote all its times down at the end of the session instead of after each buddy. Name one way the data could be wrong because of that.

3CU

You can only record three of the four things on the day. Which one would you drop, and what would you no longer be able to say about your game?

What the exit ticket tells you

Question 1: the third one is the trap. Minutes is continuous even though students will write a whole number, because the value is measured rather than counted. Students who call it discrete are classifying the way it was written down, not what it is.

Question 2 is a rehearsal for Part B item B2. Expect “you might forget”; press for the consequence — the times become estimates, all of them probably too round, and the shape of the distribution in Lesson 13 flattens out at numbers ending in 0 and 5.

Question 3 has no correct answer and sorts the class quickly. Dropping the outcome costs the link back to Part A probabilities. Dropping the time costs the whole of Lesson 13. A student who names what is lost has understood that each variable earns its place.

Where this sits in the achievement standard

This lesson is the planning half of AC9M6ST03 — plan and conduct statistical investigations, including acquiring data. It also introduces the vocabulary that AC9M6ST01 depends on: nominal and ordinal categorical, discrete and continuous numerical.

In the folio it is the source of item B1 (name each variable and identify its type) and item B2 (describe how the data was collected and identify one limitation). Both of those are answerable only by students who made the decisions themselves, which is why the lesson runs before Games Day rather than after it.

After Games Day, before Lesson 10

Collect every paper sheet on the day. Do not leave them with the teams over a weekend. Photograph or photocopy them before anything else — Lesson 10 deliberately works with messy data, and a lost sheet is a different problem from a messy one.
