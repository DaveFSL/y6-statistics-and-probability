Learning intention

We are learning to design a chance game for a five-year-old, and to work out and justify its probabilities.

Success criteria

  • I can design a game that meets every rule in the brief.
  • I can list every possible outcome of my game.
  • I can write each outcome's probability as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage.
  • I can give useful critique to another team, and change my own design because of critique I received.

Curriculum

AC9M6P01 — assign probabilities using common fractions, percentages and decimals.
AC9M6P02 — run simulations with an increasing number of trials using digital tools; compare observations with expected results.

Whatever a team designs today is what they are assessed on next lesson. Lesson 8 is Part A of the folio, and every item in it refers to "your team's game". A team that leaves today without a finished, working game cannot sit the assessment. That is the one thing to protect if the lesson runs short.
The planner is the assessment in rehearsal. Page 2 of the worksheet asks for the sample space, then the probabilities three ways, then a simulation, then a fairness judgement — the same sequence, in the same order, as assessment items A1, A2, A5 and A7. That is deliberate. Students who complete the planner properly have already done Part A once, with their team and with your help.

The Catholic perspective is not decoration here. The brief is judged by whether it serves a five-year-old, not by how clever the mathematics is. A team with an elegant game a Prep cannot play has failed the brief. Say that plainly — it is the clearest expression in the unit of putting skill at the service of someone else.

Not sure? Listing EVERY outcome is the rule teams most often break — and it is the one the assessment depends on.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs. The assessment has questions at all three levels.

SF — simple familiar

You have seen this kind of question before and it is clear what to do. One step, friendly numbers.

CF — complex familiar

You know the method, but there is more than one step, or you have to pick which method to use.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the method. You work out what to do, and explain why you did it.

A CU question is not a harder sum. It is usually one where you have to decide something and then justify it. Getting a number is not enough.

Warm-up · 5 minutes

A die where red comes up half the time

A toy maker wants to make a die where the chance of landing on red is exactly one half. What might the die look like?

Whiteboards, individual. Collect three or four different answers before saying anything.

Expect three of the faces coloured red, and accept it quickly. Then push: "does it have to be a six-sided die?" Four faces with two red works. Eight with four works. Students have met this shape three times now — Lesson 2's spinners, Lesson 3's decks, and now this — and today is where it becomes a design tool rather than an exercise.

The link to make out loud: you get to choose the sample space. That is what designing a game is.

Launch · 12 minutes

The brief

Hand out the design brief. Read it together. Every rule is non-negotiable, and each one has a reason worth two minutes of discussion.

Design brief — a chance game for your Prep buddy

  1. No reading. Your buddy may not be able to read yet. Nothing in the game can depend on words.
  2. Under two minutes a turn. Sixty Preps have to get through it.
  3. Understood after one demonstration. You show them once, then they play.
  4. The outcome must depend on chance. Not on skill, not on speed, not on strength.
  5. You must be able to list every possible outcome. If you cannot list them, you cannot work out the probabilities.

Two questions worth the discussion time

Why is a game that is too hard not a kind game?
Why does rule 4 exist? What would be wrong with a game where the best player wins?
Rule 4 is the one to labour. Students default to designing competitions — throwing, aiming, racing. A skill game is a perfectly good game and completely wrong for this unit, because it has no sample space and therefore no probabilities. It is also less kind: a five-year-old competing against a Year 6 on skill loses every time, whereas chance gives them a real go.

Rule 5 is the assessment in disguise. If a team cannot list every outcome, item A1 is impossible and everything after it collapses. Check this before a team gets attached to their idea — a game with an unlistable sample space needs redesigning at minute five, not minute twenty-five.

On prizes. Teams will want everyone to win. Let them think it through: if everyone wins, the outcome does not depend on chance, and rule 4 fails. The productive version is that everyone gets something and the chance element decides which something. That distinction is worth surfacing in the Summarise.

Explore · 26 minutes

Design it, then prove it works

Family Group teams of two or three, assigned by you. Roles named before they start: Designer, Recorder, Simulator.

The sequence on the planner

  1. Describe and sketch the game. What does the buddy actually do?
  2. List every outcome. SF If the list is not complete, stop and fix the game.
  3. Probabilities, three ways. SF Fraction, decimal, percentage, for every outcome. They must total 1.
  4. Simulate it. CF Build the game in the Chance Simulator and run it. Does the result match what you calculated?
  5. Judge it. CU Is this a good game for a five-year-old? Use the brief and your numbers.
Check the totals before anything else. The fastest way to find a broken sample space is to add the probabilities. If they do not come to 1, an outcome is missing or double-counted. Make this the first thing you check at every table.

Peer critique

Teams swap planners and use the critique protocol cards: two things that work, one question, one suggestion — judged against the brief, not against taste. Then teams revise. The revision box on the planner must be filled in even if the answer is "we changed nothing, because…".

Circulating priorities, in order.

1. Does the sample space total 1? Thirty seconds per team, and it catches most structural faults.
2. Can they list the outcomes at all? A team that says "it depends how hard you throw it" has a skill game and needs redirecting now.
3. Is a Prep going to enjoy losing this? A game with a 1-in-20 win chance is mathematically fine and miserable to play.

What good critique sounds like. Not "it's good" — the protocol cards have an "instead of / try" side for exactly this. The useful critique in this lesson is usually about rule 3: another team can tell you in ten seconds whether they understood your game from one demonstration, and that is information you cannot get from inside your own team.

Differentiation

Support

  • Choose one of three starter structures and adapt it rather than inventing from blank: a 4-section spinner, a bag of 8 counters, or a die with coloured faces.
  • Sample space table pre-drawn with the right number of rows.
  • Conversion mat on the desk.
  • Simulate at one scale only.

Core

  • Design from the brief.
  • Full sample space and probabilities three ways.
  • Simulate, critique, revise.

Extension

  • You have 24 prizes and 60 Preps will play. What probability of winning would make 24 prizes exactly right? (AC9M7P01)

Talk it through with a partner

Show your partner your answer to question 5. Your partner picks one of these to ask you:

  • Could you explain this game to a Prep in one demonstration?
  • How many of the 60 buddies would go home with nothing?
  • Does the outcome really depend on chance, or on skill?

Write down the question you were asked and what you said back.

Choose your path

Pick the one that matches where you are right now. You can change your mind.

Start from one of these and change it to make it yours. You do not have to invent a game from nothing — adapting one counts.
ASF

A 4-section spinner. Shade one section as the winning outcome. P(win) = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.

BSF

A bag of 8 counters. Make 2 of them gold. P(win) = 2/8 = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.

CSF

A die with coloured faces. Colour 3 faces red. P(red) = 3/6 = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%.

Pick one, change the numbers, then work out the new probabilities yourself.

Keep going. Design from the brief, list every outcome, work out all the probabilities three ways, simulate it, then swap planners for critique. The revision box must be filled in even if you changed nothing.
★1CU

You have 24 prizes and 60 Preps will play. What probability of winning would make 24 prizes exactly right?

★2CU

Design the prize allocation so you are unlikely to run out. How many spare do you need, and why?

★1 24 out of 60 is 24/60 = 2/5 = 0.4, so a 2 in 5 chance of winning.
★2 24 is the expected number, not a guarantee — on the day you might get more. A few spare covers the variation. Students who connect this back to Lesson 4 (observed rarely equals expected) are reasoning at the A standard.

Summarise · 8 minutes

What did you change?

Hands up if your team changed something after the critique. What did you change, and what made you change it?

Take three or four. Do not take the games themselves — take the revisions.

The revision is the learning, not the design. A first draft that survives critique untouched usually means the critique was polite rather than useful. Celebrate the teams who changed something, and ask the teams who changed nothing what question they were asked.

This is also the moment to name what happens next: the game they now have is the game they are assessed on. Any team still unhappy with theirs should say so now, not next lesson.

Three things to land

  1. Design decisions have probability consequences. Adding one section to a spinner changes every probability in the game.
  2. The probabilities must total 1. If they do not, an outcome is missing.
  3. The brief is the judge, not our taste. A game is good here if a five-year-old can play it and enjoy it.
Before they leave. Every team's planner needs a complete sample space and a full set of probabilities. Collect them, check them tonight, and hand them back at the start of Lesson 8 — students need them for Part A.

Review · 4 minutes

Exit ticket

Another team designed this game. A bag holds 10 counters. Nine are blue and ONE is gold. The buddy draws one counter. Gold wins a prize.
1SF

What is the probability of winning? Write it as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage.

2CF

If 60 Preps each play once, how many would you expect to win? Show your working.

3CU

Is this a good game for Prep buddies? Use the brief AND your numbers to explain.

Why someone else's game. Students spent the lesson inside their own design and cannot see it clearly. Judging a stranger's game is both a fairer check and the exact skill item A7 asks for.

Answers.
1. 1/10 = 0.1 = 10%.
2. 6. Method 1: 60 ÷ 10 = 6, then × 1 = 6. Method 2: 0.1 × 60 = 6.
3. There is no single right answer, and the reasoning is the whole item. A strong response connects the number to the brief: "Only about 6 out of 60 buddies would win, so 54 would go away with nothing. It passes rules 1 to 5 but it is not kind." A response that says "yes because it is fair" has not used the numbers. A response that says "no, 10% is too low" without saying what that means for 60 children has used the numbers but not the brief.

The discriminator is question 3, and it is the closest thing in the unit to item A7. Students who translate 10% into "54 children win nothing" are reasoning at the A standard.

Support version. Two gold counters in ten, so 2/10 = 1/5 = 0.2 = 20%, and 12 of the 60 would win. The judgement is the same and the arithmetic is easier.

Misconceptions to expect today

Designing a skill game

Throwing, aiming, racing. The most common failure, and it kills the mathematics because there is no sample space.

Response. "List every possible outcome for me." When they cannot, the problem is visible without you naming it. Then offer a starter structure.

"Everyone should win"

Kind instinct, broken game. If every outcome wins, the result does not depend on chance and rule 4 fails.

Response. Separate winning from receiving. Everyone gets something; chance decides which something. The game stays kind and stays a chance game.

Probabilities that do not total 1

Usually a missing outcome — the "nothing happens" case, or a section of the spinner nobody counted.

Response. Add them up in front of the team. The gap tells you what is missing and how big it is.

A win probability so low the game is miserable

A 1-in-20 chance is mathematically correct and no fun for a five-year-old. Students often do not connect the fraction to the experience.

Response. "Sixty Preps will play. How many go home with nothing?" The number does the work.
