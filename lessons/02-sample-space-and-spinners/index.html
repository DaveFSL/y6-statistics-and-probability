Sample space and spinners

AC9M6P01 50–60 minutes Term 3, Week 9
Shows teaching notes, misconceptions and answers
Students: use the tabs to move through the lesson. Everything you need to work on is on the Explore tab.

Learning intention

We are learning to work out a probability from the sample space, and to say what the numerator and denominator each mean.

Success criteria

  • I can count all the possible outcomes and all the favourable ones.
  • I can write the probability as a fraction, then convert it to a decimal and a percentage.
  • I can explain what the top number and the bottom number of the fraction each represent.
  • I can show that two spinners that look different can have the same probability.

Curriculum

AC9M6P01 — recognise that probabilities lie on numerical scales of 0–1 or 0%–100% and use estimation to assign probabilities that events occur in a given context, using common fractions, percentages and decimals.

What changes from Lesson 1. Yesterday students placed probabilities on a scale. Today they generate them from a sample space. The move is from "where does this sit?" to "where does this number come from?" — and the answer is: count the outcomes.

The lesson also opens a question and deliberately refuses to answer it. Students will spin 20 times and get a result that does not match the probability they calculated. Let that sit. It is the engine of Lessons 4, 5 and 6, and answering it today wastes it.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs. The assessment has questions at all three levels.

SF — simple familiar

You have seen this kind of question before and it is clear what to do. One step, friendly numbers.

CF — complex familiar

You know the method, but there is more than one step, or you have to pick which method to use.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the method. You work out what to do, and explain why you did it.

A CU question is not a harder sum. It is usually one where you have to decide something and then justify it. Getting a number is not enough.

Warm-up · 5 minutes

Seventy-five per cent of what?

An event has a 75% chance of happening. What could the event be?

Think alone, then share with a partner, then collect four or five on the board. Push for variety: something with a spinner, something with counters, something from real life.

What this is setting up. Students will mostly produce three-out-of-four situations, which is exactly the structure they need in the Launch. Some will offer weather or sport, where 75% is an estimate rather than a count — take those too, and note the difference briefly. Lesson 1 established that both are legitimate.

Listen for the student who says "three quarters" without connecting it to 75%. That is a conversion gap and the conversion mat should be on their desk from the start of Explore.
Quick link back. Ask one student to write 75% as a fraction and a decimal on the board while the others are still sharing. It takes ten seconds and it re-activates yesterday.

Launch · 12 minutes

Thirteen spinners on the floor

Spread the thirteen spinner cards face up on the floor with students in a circle around them. Do not hand out the worksheet yet.

Two of the thirteen. They look different. They are not.

The questions, in this order

  1. What do you notice? Take everything. Do not steer.
  2. Is the chance of landing on shaded the same for all of them?
  3. Which one gives you the smallest chance of shaded? The largest?
  4. Put them in order, smallest chance to largest. Students move the cards physically.
  5. Only once they are stuck: what would help you decide between these two?
The ordering task is built to break down, and that is the design. The thirteen spinners are 0, 1/8, 1/6, 1/4, 1/3, 3/8, 1/2, 5/8, 2/3, 3/4, 5/6, 7/8 and 1. The ends sort themselves instantly. The middle does not.

Six of the pairs sit within about 0.04 of each other — 1/8 and 1/6, 1/3 and 3/8, 5/8 and 2/3, 5/6 and 7/8. You cannot order those by eye. That is the moment the class needs a number, and it should come from them rather than from you. Wait for it.

Two cards have the same probability written differently (1/4 and 2/8). Do not point this out. If a group notices, make them the experts for the Explore. If nobody notices, it comes up in the Summarise and lands harder.
Do not let this run long. Twelve minutes. The ordering does not have to be finished or correct — leaving it unresolved sends students into Explore with a reason to calculate.

Explore · 25 minutes

Build one, work it out, then spin it

Pairs. Each pair takes a blank spinner template — the templates are pre-divided into 4, 6 or 8 equal sections — and shades however many sections they choose.

Three steps, in order

  1. Shade it. Choose how many sections to shade. Record how many sections altogether and how many shaded.
  2. Work it out. Write the probability of landing on shaded as a fraction, then a decimal, then a percentage.
  3. Spin it. Paperclip and pencil point. Twenty spins, tally the shaded ones, write the result as a fraction out of 20.
Then stop. Students will notice that step 3 does not match step 2. Write the question on the board — why doesn't it match? — and leave it there. Do not answer it. It is the whole of Lessons 4, 5 and 6.

Class spinner line

As pairs finish, they peg their spinner on a washing line across the room in order of probability. Because the templates differ, the class ends up with a genuine spread — and with at least two spinners that look different but land in the same place.

Engineer the collision. Give one pair a 4-section template and another pair an 8-section template, and quietly suggest to each that they shade "about a quarter". When both arrive at the line and want the same peg, you have the lesson's key idea handed to you by students. Hold it for the Summarise rather than resolving it at the line.

Circulating. The thing to check is not the fraction — most will get that. Check that they can say what the denominator counts. Ask: "what does the 8 tell me?" A student who says "the sections" has it; a student who says "the answer" does not.

Complexity bands on the worksheet

SF Count the sections, write the probability three ways. Sample space is visible and given.

CF Compare two spinners with different numbers of sections; find the probability of not shaded.

CU Design a spinner to a given probability, then design a different one with the same probability — and say what they have in common.

Extension

  • Design a spinner with P(shaded) exactly 3/8. Then one where it is 0.375. Same spinner or not?
  • Can you make a spinner with 8 equal sections where P(shaded) = 1/3? Explain.
  • How many of the thirteen Launch spinners could be redrawn with 24 sections?

Part C · Show your thinking

1SF

A spinner has 8 equal sections. 3 sections are shaded. Write the probability of landing on shaded three ways.

2CF

The same spinner. What is the probability of landing on a section that is NOT shaded? Explain how you worked it out.

3CF

Spinner P has 4 sections with 1 shaded. Spinner Q has 8 sections with 2 shaded. Which gives a better chance of landing on shaded? How do you know?

1 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%. The percentage is the discriminator — 3.75% or 38% means the conversion is not secure.
2 5/8 = 0.625 = 62.5%. Either count the 5 unshaded sections, or do 1 − 3/8. Ask the counters whether they could have done it without counting.
3 Neither. 1/4 and 2/8 are the same number. The spinners look different and the chance is identical.

Talk it through with a partner

Show your partner your answer to question 3. Your partner picks one of these to ask you:

  • How do you know they are the same and not just close?
  • Could you draw a third spinner with the same chance again?
  • What would change if spinner Q had 3 shaded instead of 2?

Write down the question you were asked and what you said back.

★1CU

Design a spinner where the probability of shaded is exactly 3/8. Now design a different one where it is 0.375. Is it the same spinner?

★2CU

Can you make a spinner with 8 equal sections where the probability of shaded is exactly 1/3? Explain your answer.

★1 Yes — 3/8 and 0.375 are the same number, so any spinner that works for one works for the other. Students who draw two different spinners have not connected the representations.
★2 No. A third of 8 is 2.67 sections, and you cannot shade part of a section. It only works when the number of sections is a multiple of 3.

Summarise · 8 minutes

Where the number comes from

Three things to land

  1. The denominator counts every possible outcome. The numerator counts the ones you want. Say it with a spinner in your hand, pointing at the sections.
  2. Spinners that look different can have the same probability. Hold up the 4-section-with-1-shaded and the 8-section-with-2-shaded. 1/4 and 2/8 are the same number.
  3. The probability comes from the sample space, not from what happened when you spun. This is the sentence to write on the board and keep.
Everyone spun twenty times. Did anybody get exactly the number they calculated? Why not?

Collect two or three theories. Do not settle it. Write the best version of the question on butcher's paper and leave it on the wall until Lesson 4.

Holding a question open is a teaching move, not an omission. Say so out loud: "That is a real question and we are going to answer it properly in a few lessons. I am leaving it up there." Year 6 students accept this readily when it is named, and become irritated when a question just quietly disappears.

The theories you will get are usually: not enough spins, the paperclip is dodgy, bad luck, or the spinner is not really equal. Three of those four are worth revisiting in Lesson 6, so write them down verbatim with the students' names next to them.

Vocabulary

Add to the Frayer boards: sample space. Complete this one together — it is the most abstract word in the cycle and the definition students write today will be reused in Lesson 7 and in assessment item A1.

A usable definition. The sample space is the list of everything that could possibly happen. Not what did happen. Not what you want to happen. Everything that could.

Review · 5 minutes

Exit ticket

Maya’s spinner has 10 equal sections and 4 of them are shaded. Maya spun it 10 times and it landed on shaded 7 times.
1SF

What is the probability of landing on shaded? Write it as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage.

2CU

Maya says "the probability of shaded is 7 out of 10, because that is what I spun." Is Maya right? Explain your answer.

3CF

Draw a spinner with a DIFFERENT number of sections that has the same probability of shaded as Maya’s.

Why this is not a repeat of Part C. The worksheet used spinners of 4 and 8 sections. The exit ticket uses 10, so the conversion is fresh ground rather than a second attempt at the same numbers. More importantly, question 2 tests the idea this lesson is actually built on — that the probability comes from the spinner, not from the spinning — which Part C does not touch at all.

Answers.
1. 4/10 = 0.4 = 40%. Accept 2/5. A student who simplifies to 2/5 and still gets 0.4 has the stronger grasp; one who writes 2/5 = 0.25 has simplified correctly and then converted the wrong fraction.
2. No. 7 out of 10 is what Maya observed when she spun it. The probability is 4/10, and it comes from the spinner itself — the sections did not change because of what happened when she spun. Maya's result is evidence, not the probability.
3. Any equivalent with a different total: 2 of 5, 6 of 15, 8 of 20. A student who draws 4 of 10 again has answered a different question. Support version: 5 of 10 = 1/2, so 1 of 2, 2 of 4, 3 of 6 all work.

What to sort for. Question 2 is the discriminator, and it matters more than question 3. A student who agrees with Maya has swapped observed frequency for probability — the same confusion Sam carries in the Lesson 4 exit ticket, and the one that makes Lesson 6 impossible. Note those students now; they will need reaching twice.

Question 3 sorts for readiness: students producing 2 of 5 or 8 of 20 have grasped that the sample space can be resized without changing the probability, and are ready for the extending prompts in Lesson 3.

Standing adjustments are on the linked Personalised Adjustments document and are not repeated here.

Misconceptions to expect today

"More sections means a better chance"

Students read a bigger denominator as a bigger probability. It shows up in the Launch ordering — the 8-section spinners get grouped together regardless of how many sections are shaded.

Response. Two spinners side by side: 8 sections with 1 shaded, and 4 sections with 3 shaded. Which would you rather spin? The 8-section one has more sections and a much worse chance.

1/4 and 2/8 treated as different probabilities

Predictable, and it is the point of the lesson rather than a failure. Students see two different-looking spinners and assume two different chances.

Response. Physical, not symbolic. Lay the 8-section spinner over the 4-section one and let them see the two shaded eighths cover the one shaded quarter exactly.

"The probability is 6 out of 20 because that's what I spun"

The observed result is taken as the probability. This is the misconception the whole cycle is built to address, and today it should be named but not resolved.

Response. Ask what they would have said if they had spun it only twice. Then leave it. Lesson 4 does this properly.

Counting the shaded sections but not all the sections

A student writes 3 as the probability, or 3/5 when there are 8 sections and 5 are unshaded — comparing shaded to unshaded rather than shaded to total.

Response. "Show me the 8 on your spinner." Have them touch each section as they count. The denominator has to be pointed at, not recalled.
