What might the deck be?

AC9M6P01 50–60 minutes Term 3, Week 9 Withdrawal-safe
Shows teaching notes, misconceptions and answers
Students: use the tabs to move through the lesson. Everything you need to work on is on the Explore tab.

Learning intention

We are learning to work backwards from probabilities to build a sample space, and to find more than one answer that works.

Success criteria

  • I can build a deck that matches the probabilities I am given.
  • I can find more than one deck that works.
  • I can explain why some deck sizes are impossible.
  • I can work out the probability of the outcome nobody mentioned.

Curriculum

AC9M6P01 — recognise that probabilities lie on numerical scales of 0–1 or 0%–100% and use estimation to assign probabilities that events occur in a given context, using common fractions, percentages and decimals.

What this lesson is for. Lessons 1 and 2 went forwards: here is a situation, find the probability. Today runs backwards: here are the probabilities, build a situation. That reversal is where AC9M6P01 stops being a procedure and starts being understanding, and it is the structure of assessment item A1 — students will have to construct a sample space for a game they invented, not read one off a page.

It is also a Peter Sullivan-style open task. There is no single answer, and the number of cards is deliberately not given. The withheld information is the task.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs. The assessment has questions at all three levels.

SF — simple familiar

You have seen this kind of question before and it is clear what to do. One step, friendly numbers.

CF — complex familiar

You know the method, but there is more than one step, or you have to pick which method to use.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the method. You work out what to do, and explain why you did it.

A CU question is not a harder sum. It is usually one where you have to decide something and then justify it. Getting a number is not enough.

Warm-up · 5 minutes

A one-in-four chance of what?

The probability of an event is 1/4. What might the event be? Show it on a number line.

Individual, on whiteboards. Collect four or five that use different equipment — a spinner, a bag, a die, something from real life.

This is a rehearsal for the whole lesson. The warm-up is the same reversal as the main task, just smaller and with one constraint instead of two. If students struggle here, they will struggle with the Launch, so spend the extra minute.

Watch for the student who writes 1/4 on the number line but cannot invent an event. That student needs the four-card entry point in Explore, not the eight.
Push once. When someone offers a four-section spinner with one shaded, ask: "can you make one with a probability of 1/4 that does not have four sections?" That question is the whole of Lesson 2's exit ticket, and it primes today.

Launch · 8 minutes

The mystery deck

I have a deck of cards. Every card is either a picture card or a numbered card.

If I draw one card without looking:
— the probability of a picture card is 1/4
— the probability of an even number is 1/2

What might my deck be?
K 2 8 7

One deck that works. There are many others.

Do not say how many cards are in the deck. Students will ask, repeatedly. The answer is "that's part of what you're working out." The missing number is the task — hand it over and the lesson collapses into arithmetic.

Set the rules of the deck

Agree these as a class before students start, and write them on the board:

  • Picture cards are J, Q, K. They have no number.
  • Numbered cards run from 1 to 10. Odd numbers are 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. Even numbers are 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.
  • Cards may repeat. You can have three 4s if you want.
  • Every card is either a picture card or a numbered card. Nothing is both.
Nail down the Ace before anyone starts. If you use real playing cards, someone will ask whether an Ace is a picture card or a 1. Decide as a class — recommend treating it as 1, so it is odd — and write the decision on the board. Unresolved, it produces two students with correct decks who think one of them is wrong.

Better: use the blank card templates. Students write their own values, which removes the Ace problem entirely and means you do not need fifteen real decks.

Explore · 27 minutes

Think alone first

Four steps, in this order

  1. Alone, five minutes. Each student writes down one deck that they think works. No talking. This matters — pairs that start talking immediately produce one idea between two people.
  2. In pairs. Compare. Are they the same? Are both right? Can both be right?
  3. Build it. Make the deck physically with the card blanks.
  4. Test it. Draw a card, record whether it is a picture card, put it back. Twenty times.
With replacement. The card goes back every time. If it does not, the probability changes after every draw and the test means nothing. Say this out loud and check it as you circulate — it is the most common procedural error in the lesson.

The class board

As pairs finish, they write their deck on the board as three numbers: picture / even / odd, and the total. Do not sort them. Let the list build up messily.

PictureEvenOddTotalValid?
1214yes — the smallest one
2428yes
36312yes
510520yes
2406no — 2 out of 6 is not 1/4
The last row is the one to hope for. A pair will produce a deck with no odd cards, because the problem never mentioned odd cards. It fails, and working out why it fails is the most valuable thirty seconds in the lesson: the two given probabilities add to 3/4, so a quarter of the deck has to be something else.

If nobody produces it, put it on the board yourself as "a deck someone tried in another class" and ask whether it works.

Circulating. Ask every pair the same question: "could your deck have been bigger?" Students who say yes and can produce the next size up have the generalisation. Students who say "no, this is the answer" have solved an arithmetic problem rather than an open one.

Complexity bands on the worksheet

SF Build one deck that satisfies both probabilities and record it.

CF Find the probability of drawing an odd-numbered card — the outcome the problem never mentioned. Then test whether a given deck size is possible.

CU Explain why every deck that works has a total that is a multiple of 4.

Differentiation

Support

  • One constraint at a time: build a deck with P(picture) = 1/4 first, then add the even-number rule.
  • Start with exactly 4 cards, then double it.
  • Card blanks pre-cut.
  • Conversion mat on the desk.

Core

  • Both constraints from the start.
  • Find at least two decks of different sizes.
  • Full worksheet including the multiple-of-4 explanation.

Extension

  • A deck has exactly 20 cards. How many of each kind? Is there more than one answer?
  • If 60 Prep students each drew one card and put it back, how many picture cards would you expect? (AC9M7P01)
  • A different deck has P(picture) = 1/3 and P(even) = 1/2. Which deck sizes work now, and what is P(odd)?

Part D · Show your thinking

1CF

Nobody told you about the odd-numbered cards. What is the probability of drawing an odd-numbered card? How do you know?

2CF

Two students both made decks that work. One used 8 cards, the other used 20. Are they both right? How do you know?

3CU

Look at every deck that works. Why is the total always a multiple of 4?

1 1/4. The two given probabilities are 1/4 and 1/2, which total 3/4. Everything must total 1, so the odd cards are the remaining 1/4. Counting the leftovers in your own deck also works — ask those students whether it would still work for a deck of 400.
2 Yes, both. 2 picture of 8 is 1/4, and 5 picture of 20 is 1/4. Different sizes, identical probabilities.
3 A quarter of the deck has to be a whole number of picture cards. You cannot have 2.5 kings, so the total must divide by 4.

  • If both are right, what is actually the same about them?

Summarise · 8 minutes

What stayed the same

Now sort the class board by total size. Put the invalid ones to one side rather than rubbing them out.

Three things to land

  1. There were many correct answers. Count them on the board. This is not a lesson where one pair won.
  2. Every deck that works is a multiple of 4. Because a quarter of the deck has to be a whole number of picture cards. You cannot have 2.5 kings.
  3. Every valid deck has the same three probabilities: 1/4, 1/2, 1/4. The decks are different sizes and the probabilities are identical. That is the point of the lesson.
Nobody told us about the odd-numbered cards. How did we work out their probability?
The invariant is the prize. Students arrive expecting the answer to be a deck. The actual answer is that the size was never determined and never mattered — the probabilities were fixed from the start. Getting a student to say that out loud is worth more than any of the individual decks.

The route to the odd cards is the complement, which is on the conversion mat: the two given probabilities are 1/4 and 1/2, which total 3/4, and everything must total 1, so the odd cards are the remaining 1/4. Some students will instead count the leftovers in their own deck. Both are correct. Ask the counters whether their method would still work for a deck of 400 cards.

Review · 5 minutes

Exit ticket

A bag of counters has a 1/5 chance of drawing a RED counter.
1CF

Give two DIFFERENT bags that would work. They must have different numbers of counters altogether.

2CU

Could the bag hold 8 counters? Explain your answer.

3CF

In one of your bags, what is the probability of drawing a counter that is NOT red?

Note the deliberate change. The exit ticket uses thirds, not quarters. A student who has genuinely grasped the structure will transfer it; a student who memorised "multiples of 4" from the Explore will write 4, 8, 12 and be wrong. This is a transfer check, not a repetition check.

Answers.
1. Any bag whose total is a multiple of 3 with a third red: 1 red of 3, 2 of 6, 3 of 9, 4 of 12. Two different sizes are required — two bags of 3 is not two answers.
2. No. 8 is not a multiple of 3, so a third of 8 is 2.67 counters, and you cannot have part of a counter. Accept any correct reasoning; the number 2.67 does not have to appear.
3. 2/3, whichever bag they chose. A student who gives a different answer for a different bag has missed the invariant and should be paired with a confident student in Lesson 4.

Going Further answers (worksheet page 2).
a. 5 picture, 10 even, 5 odd. There is only one answer for the counts — though the specific cards can vary, which is worth asking about.
b. 60 × 1/4 = 15 picture cards. Expect some students to say "15 exactly" — press them, because Lesson 4 is about to make this distinction the whole point.
c. Now the sizes are multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24. And P(odd) is 1/6, not 1/4. This is the item that separates students who found a rule from students who found the rule — the invariant depends on the constraints, and it changed.

Sorting for Lesson 4. Question 2 is the discriminator. Students who explain it cleanly are ready for the non-whole expected frequencies in Lesson 4's extending prompt.

Misconceptions to expect today

"There must be one right answer"

Students look for the deck rather than a deck, and stop as soon as they find one. Some will assume the pair next to them is wrong.

Response. Ask both pairs to test their decks. Two different decks that both pass is more convincing than anything you can say.

Forgetting the odd-numbered cards

The problem mentions picture cards and even numbers, so students build a deck of only those two kinds. The probabilities then come out wrong and they cannot see why.

Response. "Add up your two probabilities. What do you get? What do all the probabilities have to add to?" Then let them find the missing quarter.

Choosing a deck size that satisfies one constraint but not both

Six cards works for the even-number half. It fails for the picture-card quarter. Students check one and stop.

Response. Make them state both checks explicitly before posting to the board. The worksheet has a check column for exactly this.

Drawing without replacement

Students deal the cards out rather than drawing and returning. The probabilities shift with every draw.

Response. Catch it while circulating, not afterwards. Ask what the probability of a picture card is once they have already taken it out.
