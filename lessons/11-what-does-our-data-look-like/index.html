Lesson 11 · What does our data look like?

Every team now has a cleaned file. This lesson turns one column of it into a display, and then teaches the harder half: saying what the display shows. Describing a distribution is a skill in its own right, and it is the one students skip.

Continuous data waits for Lesson 13. Today is categorical and discrete only — outcome, rating, wins. The visit times need grouping into intervals before they can be displayed, and that is a lesson of its own. If a team asks about the times today, tell them they are right to notice the problem.

Describing the graph is not describing the data

“The third bar is the tallest” is a description of ink. “More buddies gave it a 3 than anything else” is a description of buddies. The gap between those two sentences is most of this lesson, and students who cannot cross it write Part B item B3 about bars.

The test to give them: could somebody who has never seen your graph understand your sentence? If the sentence needs the picture, it is not a description of the data.

Three things, every time

Mode — the most common value, which works for any type. Range — only for numerical data, and only ever two numbers wide. Shape — where the bulk sits, and whether anything is unusual. Shape is the one with no formula, which is exactly why it carries the Reasoning evidence.

What we are learning today

We are learning to build a display for one set of data, and to describe what it shows by its mode, its range and its shape.

I will know I can do it when…

  • I can choose a display that suits the type of data I have;
  • I can find the mode, and the range when the data is numerical;
  • I can describe the shape of a distribution in a sentence about the data, not about the bars;
  • I can say one thing my display shows that a list of numbers would not.

Set your goal for today

Read the success criteria above. Which one is the biggest stretch for you? Write one thing you are going to focus on.

Not sure? The third one. Almost everybody writes about the bars the first time.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs.

SF — simple familiar

Find the mode, find the range, pick the display. One step each.

CF — complex familiar

Describe a shape in words, or fix a description that is about the wrong thing.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Work out which measures survive missing data, and what a display is actually for.

Mode, range and shape are not three answers to the same question. Mode tells you the commonest. Range tells you how far apart the extremes are. Shape tells you where everybody else is, and only shape needs a sentence.

Warm-up · mode and range

Five minutes, out loud, on fingers. No writing.

1. 4, 7, 4, 9, 4, 2. What is the mode? 4. The range? 7.

2. 12, 12, 15, 15, 20. What is the mode? Two of them, 12 and 15. A set of data can have more than one.

3. Red, blue, red, green, red. What is the mode? Red. What is the range? There isn’t one — you cannot subtract colours.

4. 3, 3, 3, 3. What is the range? 0. Not “none” — zero.

Question 3 is the one to spend time on

Range needs subtraction, so it exists only for numerical data. Students who answer “red to green” are treating categories as if they sat on a scale. That is the same error that makes a line graph of favourite colours look reasonable, and it will come back in Lesson 12 when they choose a comparative display.

Question 2 matters for a different reason: students arrive certain that a set of data has exactly one mode. Two is allowed. None is possible as well, if every value appears once.

Two sentences about the same graph

Open resources/l11-03-display-practice.xlsx on the board, sheet Frequency tables. The chart is already built: how much the Prep buddies liked Treasure Bag.

“Here are two sentences about this graph. Both are true. Only one of them is any use.

A. The third bar is the tallest and the first bar is not there.

B. More buddies gave Treasure Bag a 3 than anything else, and nobody at all gave it a 1.

What is the difference? … Sentence A is about the picture. Sentence B is about the buddies. If I read sentence A to someone who has not seen the graph, they learn nothing.”

The test: could somebody who has never seen your graph understand your sentence? If it only makes sense next to the picture, it is a description of the picture.

Then show them where the numbers came from

Click a cell in the frequency table and point at the formula bar. The graph is drawn from the frequency table, and the frequency table counts the raw rows. Students who think a graph is drawn from the raw data cannot explain why their chart has the wrong number of bars, which is the most common thing to go wrong in the next twenty minutes.

A title and two axis labels, every time

The chart on screen has a title that says what it shows and axes that say what was measured. Make that the standard from the first chart today. In Lesson 14 the whole lesson is about displays that mislead, and a class that has never labelled an axis has no idea what a truncated one looks like.

Team Comet’s three distributions

Work from resources/l11-03-display-practice.xlsx, sheet Frequency tables. The counts are already there. The describing is the work.
1SF

Team Comet’s ratings were: two buddies gave 2, six gave 3, five gave 4. What is the mode?

2SF

Their shortest visit was 33 seconds and their longest was 44. What is the range?

3SF

For “outcome of the first turn” — Gold, Silver or Blue — would you use a column graph or a line graph? Why?

4CF

Write one sentence describing the shape of Team Comet’s ratings. Naming the mode is not enough.

5CF

Their wins out of three were: four buddies won 0, five won 1, four won 2, one won 3. Describe this in two sentences — one about where most of the data sits, one about what is unusual.

6CF

A student writes “the blue bar is shorter than the gold bar”. Why is that not a description of the data? Rewrite it so it is.

7CU

Comet has 15 buddies but only 13 visit times. Suppose the two missing times had been 20 seconds and 90 seconds. What happens to the range? What happens to the mode? Which of the two is more fragile, and why?

8CU

Your display has to answer one question for somebody who was not at Games Day. Write that question, then say what your display shows about it.

1. 3. Six buddies, more than any other rating.

2. 11 seconds. Watch for “33 to 44” — that is the spread described, not the range, and the range is a single number.

3. Column graph. Gold, Silver and Blue have no order, so a line between them would claim a trend that does not exist. If a student says “because it is categorical”, push for the reason: a line means the value in between means something, and there is nothing between Gold and Silver.

4. Something like: most buddies enjoyed it, with the ratings bunched at the top end — nobody gave it a 1, and only two gave it a 2. The marker of a good answer is that it says where the bulk is AND what is absent.

5. Expected: most buddies won either 0, 1 or 2 turns, spread fairly evenly. Only one buddy out of fourteen won all three, which stands out. The second sentence is the one that separates a description from a recitation.

6. It describes the picture, not the buddies. Rewritten: fewer buddies got Blue on their first turn than got Gold. Then push once more — is that surprising? Gold has more sections, so it should happen more often, which is Part A arriving back.

7. The one that does the work. Range goes from 11 seconds to 70 — it more than sextuples, because the range is built from exactly two values and both of them have just been replaced. The mode stays 40, because 40 still appears more often than anything else. So the range is the fragile one: two missing values can change it completely, while the mode depends on the bulk of the data. This is why Lesson 10’s blanks mattered.

8. No set answer. Strong responses pick a question somebody would actually ask — did the Preps enjoy it?, was it too hard to win? — and then answer it from the display. Weak responses write a question the display cannot answer, such as which game was best?, which needs a second team’s data. Say so: that is Lesson 12.

Pick one column from your own cleaned file — outcome, rating or wins. Build its frequency table, insert a column graph, give it a title that says what it shows and two labelled axes. Then write three sentences: the mode, the range if your data is numerical, and the shape.

Do not pick the visit times today. They need grouping first, and that is Lesson 13.

Both of these are about what the mode hides. They are the ideas Lesson 12 opens with.
★1CU

Two teams both have a mode of 3 for their ratings, but their distributions look nothing alike. Invent a set of ratings for each team to show how that can happen.

★2CU

Give an example of a set of data where reporting only the mode would give somebody the wrong impression. Say what you would report instead.

Three sentences, not three numbers

“Everybody read me your shape sentence. Not the mode — the shape.”

“Listen for whether the sentence is about buddies or about bars. If I can understand it without looking at your screen, it is a description of your data.”

WhatWhen it worksWhat it does not tell you
ModeAny type of data, including categories.Nothing about the rest of the data. Two very different sets can share a mode.
RangeNumerical data only.Where the data sits. It is built from the two most extreme values and nothing else.
ShapeAlways, and it needs a sentence rather than a number.It is not precise. That is the trade — it is the only one that describes everybody.
The range is fragile. Team Comet’s two missing times would have taken it from 11 seconds to 70. The mode would not have moved. Worth saying out loud, because it is the clearest reason this unit spent a whole lesson on blanks.

Collect one display from each team before they close the file

A screenshot in a shared folder, or printed. Lesson 12 opens by putting two of them side by side, and a display that exists only inside a student’s unsaved spreadsheet will not be there on the day.

What to check tonight

Titles and axis labels. A chart with a legend that says “Series 1” and no axis titles is the default Excel gives you, and it is the exact chart Lesson 14 will teach them to distrust.

Exit ticket

On your own. Five minutes.
1SF

Nine buddies rated a game: 4, 3, 4, 2, 4, 3, 1, 4, 3. What is the mode? What is the range?

2CF

Write one sentence describing the shape of those nine ratings. It must be about buddies, not bars.

3CU

Someone reports only “the mode was 4”. Name one thing about those nine ratings that this hides.

The answers

1. Mode 4, which appears four times. Range 3, from 1 to 4. Students who write “1 to 4” have described the spread rather than given the range.

2. Looking for something like most buddies liked it, with ratings bunched at 3 and 4, but one buddy did not enjoy it at all. The single 1 is the detail that separates a real description from a restatement of the mode.

3. That one buddy gave it a 1; that nearly as many gave a 3 as gave a 4; that nobody gave a 2 except one. Any of these. A student who says “it hides the other numbers” has the idea but not the evidence — ask which other number.

Where this sits in the achievement standard

AC9M6ST01 asks students to interpret and compare data sets for ordinal and nominal categorical and discrete and continuous numerical variables. This lesson does the interpreting half for everything except continuous, which is Lesson 13.

In the folio it produces item B3 — build a display for one categorical variable and describe it by mode and shape. The describing is what is being judged, not the chart.

Year 5 and Year 7, for the two ends of the room

A student still unsure what a column graph shows is working at the Year 5 standard — interpret and compare data displays — and the supplied frequency table is the right adjustment, not a different task. A student who has moved past shape and is asking about the middle of the data is reaching toward Year 7, where the mean and the median arrive. Name it, let them explore it, and do not make it the class task: the Year 6 standard is mode, range and shape.
