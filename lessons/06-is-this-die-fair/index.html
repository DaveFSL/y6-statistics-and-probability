Is this die fair?

Learning intention

We are learning to judge whether the evidence we have is strong enough to answer the question we are asking.

Success criteria

  • I can say what a result would look like if a die were fair.
  • I can explain why a small number of rolls cannot settle the question.
  • I can design a test: how many trials, why that many, and what result would convince me.
  • I can say "we cannot tell yet" when that is the honest answer.

Curriculum

AC9M6P02 — conduct repeated chance experiments and run simulations with an increasing number of trials using digital tools; compare observations with expected results and discuss the effect on variation of increasing the number of trials.

This is the reasoning checkpoint of the probability block. The written justifications you collect today are the best predictor you will get of how students will handle assessment item A7 — the fairness judgement, which is the only complex unfamiliar item in Part A. Read them before you plan Lesson 7.
What makes this lesson different. Every lesson so far has had an answer. Today the honest answer for most of the lesson is "we do not have enough evidence", and students find that genuinely uncomfortable. Naming that discomfort is part of the teaching: mathematicians say "not yet" all the time, and it is a stronger answer than a confident guess.

Lesson 5 established that more trials make the fraction settle. Today applies it under pressure, when there is something at stake in the answer.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs. The assessment has questions at all three levels.

SF — simple familiar

You have seen this kind of question before and it is clear what to do. One step, friendly numbers.

CF — complex familiar

You know the method, but there is more than one step, or you have to pick which method to use.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the method. You work out what to do, and explain why you did it.

A CU question is not a harder sum. It is usually one where you have to decide something and then justify it. Getting a number is not enough.

Warm-up · 6 minutes

Who has the fair die?

Two students each rolled a die twenty times.

Ana counted her sixes: she got six 6s.
Ben counted his sixes: he got none at all.

One of them has a loaded die. Which one?

Hands up for Ana. Hands up for Ben. Hands up for "cannot tell". Record the three counts on the board — you will come back to them in the Summarise.

The answer is "cannot tell", and almost nobody will choose it. Here are the actual numbers for a perfectly fair die rolled twenty times, counting only the sixes:

· expected number of 6s: 3.33
· chance of getting six or more 6s: 10.2% — about one roll-of-twenty in ten
· chance of getting no 6s at all: 2.6%

So in a class of thirteen pairs all rolling fair dice, you would expect roughly one pair to see Ana's result. Ben's is rarer but hardly impossible.

Do not give these numbers away now. Take the vote, write the three counts on the board, and move on. The numbers land properly in the Summarise, after students have felt the uncertainty themselves.
If someone says "cannot tell" straight away. Ask them what would change their mind — what result would convince them? That question is the whole Explore, and you have just been handed it by a student.

Launch · 12 minutes

The mystery pair

Open the Mystery dice tab of the Chance Simulator on the board. Two dice, A and B. One is fair. The other is weighted so that one face is three times as likely as any other. Nothing on the screen says which.

You get twenty rolls each. That is all. Which die is weighted?

Set the roll count to 20, roll both, and put the two tables side by side. Give groups two minutes to argue, then take a vote: A, B, or cannot tell.

Do not reveal. Not after the vote, not when asked, not at the end of the Launch. The reveal belongs in the Summarise and it is worth much more there.
What will actually happen, measured. Using the obvious student strategy — whichever die has the biggest single-face count is the weighted one — here is how a class performs at each roll count:

So at twenty rolls, roughly a quarter of the room is either wrong or unable to separate the two dice — and the three quarters who are right cannot tell that they are right. That is exactly the situation you want before the Explore.

The weighted face is 3/8, or 37.5%. A fair face is 1/6, about 16.7%. The gap is large — and twenty rolls still is not enough to see it reliably. Students find that genuinely surprising.

If the class votes overwhelmingly one way, ask the confident students what result would have changed their minds. Most will not have an answer, and noticing that is the point.

Explore · 24 minutes

Design a test that would actually settle it

Groups of three. The task is not to find the answer. The task is to design the test.

Before you roll anything else, write down three things:

1. How many rolls will you use?
2. Why that many?
3. What result would convince you?

Only when all three are written do groups run the test in the simulator.

Question 3 is the one that matters. A group that cannot say in advance what would convince them has not designed a test — they are just rolling until something looks obvious. Push on this while circulating.

REASONING CHECKPOINT — collect these

Every student writes their own justification on page 2 of the worksheet, even though the design was a group decision. This is the formative evidence for the whole probability block.

What a strong response looks like. Not "we used 200 rolls because more is better", but something with a number attached to the reasoning: "We used 200 because on a fair die each face should come up about 33 times, and on the weighted one it should be about 75. Those are far enough apart that we would not mix them up."

A student who can name what the fair result should look like has understood expected frequency. A student who can say how far apart the two predictions are has understood why sample size matters. Both are A-standard signals for item A7.

What to do with a group that says "1000 rolls". Ask whether they need that many. Then ask what the smallest number would be that they would still trust. Over-testing is not a mathematical error, but choosing a number without reasoning is.

Circulating question: "What would you expect to see if it were fair?" Groups that cannot answer this cannot design a test, because they have nothing to compare against. Send them back to their Lesson 4 expected-frequency table.

Part C · Show your thinking

1SF

A fair six-sided die is rolled 60 times. How many 4s would you expect? Show your working.

2CF

Two dice were each rolled 30 times. Die A showed a six 5 times. Die B showed a six 11 times. Which is more likely weighted? How sure can you be?

3CU

Explain WHY more rolls give better evidence. "More is better" is not an explanation — say what more rolls actually do.

1 10. 60 ÷ 6 = 10.
2 Die B looks more likely, since 11 is well above the 5 you would expect. But 30 rolls is still a small sample and you cannot be sure. "B, but I am not certain" is the strongest answer.
3 Each single roll has less power to move the fraction. One extra six out of 20 rolls shifts the result by 5%; out of 2000 it shifts it by 0.05%. An unlucky run gets swamped by all the ordinary results around it.

Summarise · 10 minutes

Now reveal it

Press Reveal on the simulator. Then, before any celebrating, go back to the vote you recorded in the Launch and count how many groups were right.

Everyone who was right — how did you know you were right? Could you have been sure at the time?
This is the sharpest moment in the lesson. The groups who guessed correctly at twenty rolls did not have better evidence than the groups who guessed wrongly. They had the same weak evidence and a luckier outcome. Getting that said out loud, by a student who was right, is worth more than anything you can tell them.

Then return to the warm-up and give the real numbers: a fair die produces six or more 6s in twenty rolls about 10% of the time, and no 6s at all about 2.6% of the time. Both Ana and Ben could easily have had fair dice. Nobody in the room could have known.

Three things to land

  1. A small sample cannot settle a question about fairness. Twenty rolls looks like data and behaves like noise.
  2. The evidence has to be proportionate to the claim. Saying a die is loaded is a big claim, and it needs more than a hunch and twenty rolls.
  3. "We cannot tell yet" is a real answer. It is what a mathematician says when the evidence is not there, and it is stronger than a confident guess.
How sure you are should depend on how much evidence you have. Not on how strongly you feel about it

Vocabulary

Add to the Frayer boards: variation. Students now have the best possible example for the Examples quadrant — the spread of results between groups at twenty rolls, compared with the spread at two hundred.

Review · 4 minutes

Exit ticket

Kai has a spinner with 4 equal sections. One section is red. Kai spun it 12 times and it landed on red 5 times. Kai says the spinner is rigged.
1SF

How many reds would you expect in 12 spins?

2CF

Is Kai right that the spinner is rigged? Explain your answer.

3CU

What would you do to find out for sure? Say how many spins you would use and why.

Why a spinner and not a die. The whole lesson is dice. Using a spinner forces transfer rather than recall, and the numbers are new — 12 spins, expected 3, observed 5.

Answers.
1. 3. Method 1: 12 ÷ 4 = 3, then × 1 = 3. Method 2: 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25, then × 12 = 3.
2. No — or more precisely, we cannot tell. Five is more than three, but twelve spins is a very small sample and a fair spinner would produce five reds fairly often. Accept "we cannot tell yet" as the strongest answer. Accept "no, because 12 spins is not enough" as fully correct. Do not accept "no, because 5 is close to 3" without the sample-size reasoning — that is the right answer for the wrong reason.
3. Any number with reasoning attached. Look for a student who says what the fair result would look like at their chosen scale — "if I spun it 200 times I would expect about 50 reds, so if I got 90 I would believe Kai". The number matters far less than whether a prediction comes with it.

The discriminator is question 3, not question 2. Most students will get question 2 right by now. Question 3 asks them to design, which is what item A7 asks. A student who writes a number with no reasoning has not met the success criteria, even if the number is sensible.

Support version. Same three questions, but the spinner is spun 16 times and lands on red 6 times, so the expected number is 4. The excess is smaller relative to the sample, which makes "we cannot tell" easier to reach.

Sorting for Lesson 7. Students who write a prediction into question 3 are ready to lead their Family Group team's game design. Put one in each team.

Misconceptions to expect today

"Six 6s in twenty rolls proves it's loaded"

The headline misconception. A run that feels extreme is treated as proof.

Response. The number: a fair die does that about 10% of the time. Roll twenty on the simulator ten times over and let the class watch how often something surprising turns up.

"I was right, so my method was good"

Groups that guessed correctly at twenty rolls conclude their reasoning was sound. It was not — they were lucky.

Response. Ask them to predict again with a fresh mystery pair, still at twenty rolls. A second round separates method from luck quickly.

"More rolls is better" with nothing behind it

Students repeat Lesson 5's conclusion as a slogan without the mechanism.

Response. "Better how?" Then point at the one-extra-roll table from Lesson 5. At twenty rolls, one extra six moves the fraction by 5%.

Refusing to commit to any answer

The opposite failure, and it appears after the Summarise. Students learn that "cannot tell" is safe and apply it to everything.

Response. Run 2000 rolls on the mystery pair. The evidence is overwhelming and "cannot tell" is now the wrong answer. Enough evidence means you should commit.
