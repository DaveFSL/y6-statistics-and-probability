Where does it sit?

AC9M6P01 50–60 minutes Term 3, Week 9 Diagnostic lesson
Teaching notes
Students: use the tabs to move through the lesson. Everything you need to work on is on the Explore tab.

Learning intention

We are learning to place a probability on a scale and write it three different ways.

Success criteria

  • I can put an event on a 0–1 scale and say why it sits there.
  • I can write the same probability as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage.
  • I can explain what 0 and 1 mean on the scale.
  • I can tell the difference between an event I can calculate and one I can only estimate.

Curriculum

AC9M6P01 — recognise that probabilities lie on numerical scales of 0–1 or 0%–100% and use estimation to assign probabilities that events occur in a given context, using common fractions, percentages and decimals.

Where this sits. First lesson of the cycle, and it carries the diagnostic. The mathematics is deliberately modest because the lesson's other job is to tell you who can convert between fractions, decimals and percentages — the prerequisite most likely to block AC9M6P01.

Not sure? Converting between fractions, decimals and percentages is the one most people find hardest today.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs. The assessment has questions at all three levels.

SF — simple familiar

You have seen this kind of question before and it is clear what to do. One step, friendly numbers.

CF — complex familiar

You know the method, but there is more than one step, or you have to pick which method to use.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the method. You work out what to do, and explain why you did it.

A CU question is not a harder sum. It is usually one where you have to decide something and then justify it. Getting a number is not enough.

Warm-up · 5 minutes

An even chance of what?

I heard someone say an event had an even chance of happening. What might they have been talking about?

Think on your own first, then share with a partner. There are lots of right answers.

Why this question. Open task, no single answer, sets the tone on day one. It also surfaces within five minutes whether students hear "even chance" as equally likely or as even number. Both readings will appear — park the second and let the human number line settle it.

Or instead · 15 minutes

Diagnostic pre-test

If the pre-test is running in this lesson, it replaces the warm-up. 10 items, 15 minutes, no calculator.

What you are actually looking for. Item-level accuracy matters less than the pattern in items 4 to 7, the conversion items. A student who can do 1/2 → 0.5 → 50% but not 3/8 → 0.375 → 37.5% is a Core student who needs the conversion mat, not a Support student. A student who cannot do halves and quarters needs concrete work in Lesson 2 before anything symbolic.

Read the items aloud one at a time for students who need it. Untimed for those students, and it does not gate their entry to the lesson. Analyse against the teacher analysis sheet before Lesson 2.

Launch · 12 minutes

The human number line

Clear a line across the room. 0 at one wall, 1 at the other, and a blank card at the halfway point.

Events to call out

1 The sun will rise tomorrow morning.
2 I roll a 7 on an ordinary six-sided die.
3 A tossed coin lands heads.
4 I roll an even number on a die.
5 I roll a 6 on a die.
6 It will rain in Brisbane tomorrow.
The two moments that matter.

Events 3 and 4 land in the same place. Students find this surprising — one has two outcomes, the other six. First chance to say probability is about the proportion of favourable outcomes, not the number of them.

Event 6, the rain. Somebody will say 50% because "either it rains or it doesn't". This is the equiprobability bias and the most durable misconception in the cycle. Ask "by that reasoning, what is the chance the sun rises tomorrow?" and let the room do the work.

Then relabel the line

Look at the line. What has changed? Has anything moved?
The sentence to get on the board. "A probability is a number between 0 and 1, or between 0% and 100%. It never goes outside that."

Explore · 22 minutes

Part A · Put the events on the line

Write the letter of each event where it belongs, then work out its probability three ways. Everything here is on the worksheet in the same order, so you can work on screen or on paper.

Part B · Write each probability three ways

Fraction, then decimal, then percentage. Use the conversion mat if you need it.

ASF

A tossed coin lands heads.

BSF

An ordinary six-sided die shows a 6.

CSF

An ordinary six-sided die shows an even number.

DSF

An ordinary six-sided die shows a 7.

ESF

A four-section spinner, one section shaded, lands on shaded.

FCF

A bag holds 8 counters: 2 red, 6 blue. You draw a red one.

GCF

The same bag. You draw a counter that is not red.

HCF

An eight-section spinner has 3 shaded sections. It lands on shaded.

ICF

A bag holds 5 counters: 1 red, 4 green. You draw a green one.

JCU

The next car that drives past the school gate is white.

You cannot work this one out exactly. Estimate it, then explain what you based your estimate on.

A 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%  ·  B 1/6 ≈ 0.17 ≈ 16.7%  ·  C 3/6 = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%  ·  D 0 = 0 = 0%  ·  E 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%
F 2/8 = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%  ·  G 6/8 = 3/4 = 0.75 = 75%  ·  H 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%  ·  I 4/5 = 0.8 = 80%
J No single answer. Any sensible estimate with a reason behind it.

Part C · Show your thinking

1SF

Events C and A ended up in the same place on the line. Explain why, even though a die has six outcomes and a coin has two.

2CF

Look at events F and G. Add your two answers together. What do you get? Why does that happen?

3CU

Event J — the next car past the gate is white. Write your estimate, then explain what you based it on.

1 Both are one half. On the die, 3 of the 6 outcomes are even, and 3/6 is the same as 1/2. What matters is the proportion of favourable outcomes, not how many outcomes there are.
2 1/4 + 3/4 = 1. Red and not-red cover every possible outcome, and all the probabilities in a sample space add to 1.
3 No correct number. A strong answer names what the estimate is based on — "about 1/4, because white is one of the common car colours and I see a lot of them."

Talk it through with a partner

Show your partner your answer to question 2. Your partner picks one of these to ask you:

  • Would that always happen, or just for this bag?
  • Can you show me where the 1 comes from on the number line?
  • What would the two answers add to if the bag had three colours?

Write down the question you were asked and what you said back.

Choose your path

Pick the one that matches where you are right now. You can change your mind.

S1SF

Do events A, B, C, D and E only.

S2SF

A bag holds 10 counters: 5 red, 5 blue. You draw a red one. Write the probability three ways.

S3SF

A bag holds 4 counters: 3 red, 1 blue. You draw a red one. Write the probability three ways.

S2 5/10 = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%  ·  S3 3/4 = 0.75 = 75%
Keep going. Finish every event in Part B, then all three questions in Part C. Do event J last — it needs writing, not calculating.
★1CU

Events G and I are close together. Which is more likely? Say how you know without working either one out.

★2CU

Invent an event with a probability of exactly 5/8. Write it as a decimal and a percentage too.

★3CU

Is there an event with a probability of exactly 1 that is not about the sun or the calendar?

★1 G is 6/8, three quarters. I is 4/5. Both are one piece short of the whole — G is missing a quarter, I a fifth. A fifth is smaller, so I is missing less, so I is more likely. Converting to decimals answers a different question.
★2 Any device with 5 favourable outcomes in 8. 5/8 = 0.625 = 62.5%.
★3 Yes — anything certain by definition. "I roll a number less than 7 on an ordinary die."

Summarise · 8 minutes

Pulling it together

Three things to land

  1. A probability is a number on a scale. 0 to 1, or 0% to 100%. Nothing sits outside it.
  2. 0 and 1 are not "very unlikely" and "very likely". They mean impossible and certain.
  3. The same probability can be written three ways. ½, 0.5 and 50% are the same number wearing different clothes.
Which events today could we work out exactly, and which could we only estimate?
Somebody says an event has a 120% chance of happening. What would you say back?
The calculate-versus-estimate distinction is worth the time. Both are in AC9M6P01 — the descriptor explicitly says "use estimation to assign probabilities". It is what the media critique in Lesson 14 eventually rests on.

Vocabulary

Frayer boards today: probability, outcome, impossible, certain, even chance.

EAL/D. "Even chance" is the trap — in everyday English "even" usually means an even number.

Review · 5 minutes

Exit ticket

An event has a probability of 0.2
1SF

Write 0.2 as a fraction and as a percentage.

2CF

Draw a spinner, a bag of counters or a die that would give this probability. Label it so someone else can see why it works.

3SF

Mark 0.2 on the number line with an arrow.

Answers. 1 0.2 = 1/5 = 20%. Accept 2/10. 2 Any device where one outcome in five is favourable. 3 One fifth along, clearly left of the midpoint.

Sort into three piles as you read, and do not write on them.
Pile 1 — all three correct. Core or Extension for Lesson 2.
Pile 2 — correct fraction and percentage, drawing does not match. Very common; the student can convert but has not connected the number to a physical sample space, which is what Lesson 2 does.
Pile 3 — conversion errors, or 0.2 placed past the midpoint. Conversion mat permanently on the desk.

The most useful single signal is question 3. A student who marks 0.2 to the right of ½ is reading the decimal as a whole number, and that will wreck Lesson 4 unless it is caught here.

Misconceptions to expect today

"Either it happens or it doesn't, so it's 50-50"

The equiprobability bias. Returns in Lessons 4 and 6.

Response. Apply the student's own reasoning to an event where it obviously fails — "what is the chance the sun rises tomorrow, then?"

Reading 0.25 as larger than 0.5

The student reads the digits as a whole number. It shows as a decimal placed past the midpoint.

Response. Go back to the fraction. If ¼ is one part of four and ½ is one part of two, which piece is bigger?

"Even chance" meaning "even number"

Predictable, and not only among EAL/D students.

Response. Name both meanings on the everyday-versus-mathematical chart. It is a reasonable reading of the English, not an error.

Treating 0 as "very unlikely"

Students place impossible events slightly above 0.

Response. Rolling a 7 on a six-sided die. There is no 7 on the die.
