Learning intention

We are learning to run simulations with more and more trials, and to describe what improves when we do.

Success criteria

  • I can set up a chance device in the simulator and check its sample space is right.
  • I can predict before I run, then record what the simulator gave me.
  • I can work out relative frequency: observed divided by trials.
  • I can say which number gets closer to the probability, and which one does not.

Curriculum

AC9M6P02 — conduct repeated chance experiments and run simulations with an increasing number of trials using digital tools; compare observations with expected results and discuss the effect on variation of increasing the number of trials.

Test the simulator on a school device before this lesson. Open shared/chance-simulator.html on a student laptop, not on yours. It needs no internet to work, but the font loads from Google Fonts and some filters block that — it degrades rather than breaks, but you want to know first. Assessment item A5 depends on this tool.
New word today: relative frequency. It is not on the unit's Frayer list and it should be — add a blank Frayer sheet for it. Definition to aim for: the number of times it happened, divided by the number of trials. It is the bridge between a count and a probability, and it is the column students will be judged on in assessment item A6.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs. The assessment has questions at all three levels.

SF — simple familiar

You have seen this kind of question before and it is clear what to do. One step, friendly numbers.

CF — complex familiar

You know the method, but there is more than one step, or you have to pick which method to use.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Nobody tells you the method. You work out what to do, and explain why you did it.

A CU question is not a harder sum. It is usually one where you have to decide something and then justify it. Getting a number is not enough.

Three on whiteboards

Quick retrieval from Lesson 4. Show one at a time, thirty seconds each, boards up together.

Question 3 is the setup for the whole lesson. Everyone gets 1000 easily. Then ask: "hands up if you think you would get exactly 1000." Very few hands, correctly. Then: "so how close would you get?" Nobody knows — and that is precisely what today measures. Do not answer it; move to the Launch.

Launch · 12 minutes

Two thousand trials would take all term

Do the arithmetic out loud with the class: Lesson 4's forty draws took about ten minutes. Two thousand draws would take around eight hours. Twenty thousand would take four school weeks of solid drawing.

So we are going to get a computer to do the drawing for us. It is the same experiment — just faster.

Demonstrate on the board, in this order

  1. Choose the device. Counters in a bag: 2 red, 1 blue, 1 green — the same bag as Lesson 4, so students recognise it.
  2. Check the picture. Point at the drawn bag. The picture is the sample space; if it looks wrong, the numbers will be wrong.
  3. Predict. Enter the class prediction for reds out of 20. Lock it in.
  4. Run 20. Read the observed count and the relative frequency aloud.
  5. Run 200. Then 2000. Each time, write the relative frequency on the board beneath the last one.
Write only the relative frequency column on the board. Four numbers under each other, nothing else. The pattern is invisible in a full table and obvious in a single column.
Do not run 20 000 in the Launch. Save it. When a student asks "how high does it go?" — and one will — that is the moment to run it, and the moment lands better if it is their question rather than your demonstration.

What the board should look like after the demonstration. Something close to:

20 trials → 0.55   200 trials → 0.48   2000 trials → 0.506

Ask what they notice. Somebody will say the numbers are getting closer to 0.5. Ask how they know 0.5 is the target — the answer is the bag, not the results, and that is Lesson 2 coming back.

Explore · 25 minutes

Your own device, four scales

Students work in pairs on one device, with the simulator open in one window and resources/l5-03-simulation-record.xlsx in another.

The steps are on the task-analysis strip

  1. Set up your chance device in the simulator. Check the picture matches what you meant.
  2. Type the same device into the workbook — outcome names and ways. The probability column fills itself in.
  3. Choose one outcome to follow. Type its name into the yellow cell.
  4. Predict, lock in, and run at 20. Type the observed count into the workbook.
  5. Repeat at 200, 2000 and 20 000.
  6. Look at the graph the workbook draws, then answer the four questions underneath.
Only the yellow cells are theirs to fill. Everything else is formulas. Say this once at the start and point at the legend at the top of the sheet — it saves a dozen interruptions.

One real set of results. The difference column climbs. The relative frequency column tightens. Both at once.

This table is the lesson, and it is why the workbook shows both columns. A student who watches only the difference column will conclude that more trials make things worse — and they will be right about that column. The relative frequency is the one that improves.

Circulate and ask one question: "which column is getting closer to the probability?" Not "is it getting closer" — that invites a yes from a student looking at the wrong column.

Part B · Show your thinking

1SF

Out of 250 spins, a spinner landed on red 137 times. What is the relative frequency? Give it as a decimal.

2CF

A simulator ran 2000 trials. The relative frequency came out at 0.336. What is the probability most likely to be? Explain how you decided.

3CU

Ali says more trials made it worse, because the difference got bigger. Bo says more trials made it better. They are both looking at real numbers. What is each of them looking at? Who is answering the right question?

1 137 ÷ 250 = 0.548.
2 About 1/3. 0.336 is very close to 0.333, and with 2000 trials the relative frequency has settled enough to trust. A device with 3 equal outcomes, or 2 of 6.
3 Both are right about their own column. Ali is watching the raw difference, which usually GROWS with more trials. Bo is watching the relative frequency, which settles towards the probability. Bo is answering the question that matters, because the fraction is what you compare with a probability.

Summarise · 10 minutes

Why more trials actually help

This is the explanation, and it is sayable to Year 6. Put it on the board as a question first.

If you run 20 trials and get one extra red, how much does that change your fraction? What if you run 2000 and get one extra red?
TrialsOne extra red changes the fraction by
201/20 = 0.05, which is 5%
2001/200 = 0.005, which is 0.5%
20001/2000 = 0.0005, which is 0.05%
20 0001/20 000 = 0.00005, which is 0.005%
The more trials you run, the less power any single trial has to move the answer. That is why the fraction settles down
This is the mechanism, and it is worth the ten minutes. Most Year 6 treatments stop at "more trials is better", which students accept without understanding and cannot then apply in Lesson 6. The table above turns it into arithmetic they can already do.

An unlucky run at 20 trials swings the answer wildly because each trial is worth 5% of the total. The same unlucky run inside 2000 trials is swamped by all the ordinary results around it.

The variation point. If you ran this lesson with 200 classes, the relative frequencies at 20 trials would spread from about 0.20 to 0.80. At 20 000 they would spread from about 0.489 to 0.510. That collapsing spread is what "variation" means, and it is the word Lesson 6 needs. Introduce it here if the class is with you.

Three things to land

  1. Relative frequency is observed divided by trials. It turns a count into something you can compare with a probability.
  2. As trials increase, relative frequency settles towards the probability. The raw difference does not — it usually grows.
  3. The reason is that each trial matters less. One result out of 20 000 cannot move the fraction the way one result out of 20 can.
The wall question. The butcher's paper from Lesson 2 can now come down, or be marked answered. The class has the answer: twenty spins was too few, and the fraction — not the difference — is the thing that gets closer.

Review · 4 minutes

Exit ticket

A simulator ran a fair coin 500 times and got 262 heads.
1SF

What is the relative frequency of heads? Give it as a decimal.

2CF

Is 262 heads close to what you would expect? Explain.

3CU

Jo ran the same coin 40 times and got 24 heads. Whose result is better evidence that the coin is fair? Explain your answer.

Answers.
1. 262 ÷ 500 = 0.524. Accept 0.52.
2. Expected is 250. Observed is 262, so it is 12 out of. As a fraction, 0.524 against 0.5 — close. Both readings are acceptable; a student who only says "no, it should be 250" has missed Lesson 4.
3. The 500-trial run. Jo's 24 out of 40 is a relative frequency of 0.6, which looks further from 0.5 — but the real reason is the sample size, not the number. With only 40 trials, one lucky streak moves the answer a long way. The 500-trial run had less room to be misled.

Question 3 is the discriminator and it feeds straight into Lesson 6. A student who says "Jo's, because 24 is closer to half of 40 than 262 is to half of 500" is comparing raw differences and will be defeated by the mystery dice tomorrow. Anyone giving that answer should be paired deliberately in Lesson 6.

Note that 0.6 is further from 0.5 than 0.524 is, so the numbers happen to agree with the correct reasoning here. Do not let that pass as understanding — ask which sample you would trust if Jo's 40 trials had given exactly 20.

Misconceptions to expect today

"More trials made it worse — look, the difference got bigger"

Entirely reasonable, and based on a real column. The student is watching the difference rather than the relative frequency.

Response. Do not correct it as an error. "You are right about that column. Now look at the one next to it." Both facts are true at once, and holding both is the actual learning.

"The simulator is rigged — it gave me exactly 1000"

Or the reverse: a student gets an unusual result and concludes the tool is broken.

Response. Have them run the same scale three times and compare. Three different answers from the same set-up settles it faster than any explanation.

Typing the relative frequency in as the observed count

The simulator shows both. Students enter 0.55 where the workbook wants 11, and everything downstream breaks.

Response. The workbook's observed column is formatted as a whole number, so 0.55 appears as 1. Point at the column header: it says observed, and observed is a count of things that happened.

"20 000 trials means the answer is exact now"

Students swing from "results are random" to "big samples are certain". The relative frequency at 20 000 is close, not exact, and it is still different every run.

Response. Run 20 000 twice on the board. The two answers differ in the third decimal place. Close is not the same as certain.
