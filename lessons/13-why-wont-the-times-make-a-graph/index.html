Lesson 13 · Why won’t the times make a graph?

The last of the four data types, and the one that needs a technique of its own. Thirteen visit times, twelve of them different: a column graph of the raw values would have twelve bars, nearly all of them one high, and would show nothing. Grouping into intervals is the fix, and choosing the intervals is the thinking.

If a team has fewer than about eight usable times, grouping will not work for them. That is not their fault and it should not cost them the lesson. The ruler drop data on the Resources tab is twenty-eight measurements with a clear shape, and any team can use it for today and for item B4 of the folio, saying so in item B2.

Let the failure happen

The Launch is a graph that does not work. Build it on the board from the raw times, let the class look at twelve bars of height one, and ask what it tells them. Nothing. Then group and build it again. Students who are told “continuous data has to be grouped” remember a rule; students who watch the ungrouped graph fail remember a reason.

Where does 35 go?

The boundary question is the one that separates a working set of intervals from a broken one. “30 to 35” and “35 to 40” both contain 35, so a value can be counted twice or argued about. Whole-second intervals — 30 to 33, 34 to 37 — dodge it. With decimals you need “30 up to but not including 35”, and that is the extension question, not the core.

What grouping costs

Once the times are in groups, 38 and 41 look identical. That is the trade and it should be said out loud: grouping buys you a shape and it pays for it with detail. A student who can state the trade is doing the Reasoning that item B4 is looking for.

What we are learning today

We are learning to group measurements into intervals so that they can be displayed, and to describe the shape of the grouped data.

I will know I can do it when…

  • I can explain why a graph of raw measurements shows nothing useful;
  • I can make groups that are equal width, with no gaps and no overlaps;
  • I can choose a sensible number of groups and say why;
  • I can describe the shape of my grouped data, and say what grouping has hidden.

Not sure? The last part of the last one. Saying what you have hidden is harder than saying what you can see.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs.

SF — simple familiar

Count values into groups you have been given.

CF — complex familiar

Choose a group width, check the groups work, describe the shape.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Work out what grouping costs you, and what to do when a value sits on a boundary.

There is no single right set of intervals. Several widths work, and you have to be able to say why yours is one of them. That is what makes the choosing a complex-familiar task rather than a procedure.

Warm-up · discrete or continuous?

Five minutes, out loud. The test: does half of one make sense?

1. How many buddies came to our stall. — Discrete. Half a buddy does not exist.

2. How long a buddy’s visit took. — Continuous. Half a second exists.

3. A buddy’s height. — Continuous, even though we write it as a whole number of centimetres.

4. How many turns somebody won. — Discrete.

5. Which of those four would be hardest to draw a column graph of? — The two continuous ones. Hold that thought.

Number 3 is the one worth labouring

Students classify by how the number was written down rather than by what it is. Height written as 142 looks discrete; it is a measurement rounded to the nearest centimetre, and the real value is somewhere in between. The same applies to the visit times: a stopwatch read to the nearest second is still continuous data.

This matters today because it decides whether a student groups the times or tries to tally them like a count.

A graph that does not work

Open resources/l13-03-grouping-times.xlsx, sheet Visit times. Comet’s thirteen times are in column A.

“Thirteen buddies, thirteen times. Twelve of those thirteen numbers are different from each other.

Let us graph them. Pick the column, insert a column graph … and there it is. Twelve bars. Almost every one of them exactly one high.

What does that graph tell you about how long a visit took? … Nothing. And it is not a mistake — it is what happens every time you graph measurements, because no two measurements are ever quite the same.”

The fix: stop asking how many buddies took exactly 38 seconds, and start asking how many took between 38 and 41. That is an interval, and it turns thirteen almost-unique numbers into a shape.

“Watch what happens when I group them in fours.” [Switch to the Grouping sheet. Five groups, a clear shape, and Nebula alongside.]

“Now it says something. Most of Comet’s visits took between 38 and 45 seconds. Most of Nebula’s took under 34. Nebula’s game was quicker to play.”

Build the broken graph for real

It takes forty seconds and it is the whole argument for the lesson. Do not describe it — make it, look at it together, and delete it.

Then show them the two From and To columns

The counts on the Grouping sheet are formulas reading the From and To cells. Change a boundary and the bars move. Let one student try groups of two and another try groups of ten, on the board, before anybody chooses their own width.

Grouping the visit times

Work from resources/l13-03-grouping-times.xlsx. The Grouping sheet counts for you once you have decided the boundaries — but do questions 1 and 2 on paper first, so you know what the formula is doing.
1SF

Using the groups 26–29, 30–33, 34–37, 38–41 and 42–45, how many of Comet’s visits were in each group? Check your five numbers add to 13.

2SF

Do the same for Nebula’s ten visits.

3CF

Each of those groups is four seconds wide. Why do all the groups have to be the same width? What would go wrong if one were twice as wide as the others?

4CF

A team uses the groups 30–35 and 35–40. What is wrong with those two groups? Write a pair that fixes it.

5CF

Try groups of 2 seconds, then groups of 10. What goes wrong with each? About how many groups seems to work best?

6CF

Describe the shape of the two grouped distributions in one sentence that compares them.

7CU

Once the times are grouped, 38 seconds and 41 seconds look exactly the same. What have you lost by grouping, and why is it still worth doing?

8CU

Comet had 13 times from 15 buddies and Nebula had 10 from 10. Does that change how you should compare the two grouped displays? Say what you would do about it.

1. 0, 1, 4, 5, 3 — and they add to 13. The adding-up check is not a formality: a set of intervals that does not account for every value has a gap or an overlap in it.

2. 4, 4, 1, 1, 0, adding to 10.

3. Because the height of a bar is being read as how common that length of visit is. A group twice as wide collects roughly twice as many values for free, so its bar is tall for a reason that has nothing to do with the data. Students often answer “it would look untidy”; push them to “it would look more common than it is”.

4. 35 is in both groups, so a visit of 35 seconds could be counted twice, or argued about, or missed. Fixes: 30–34 and 35–39, or “30 up to but not including 35”. Accept either; the second is what a statistician writes.

5. Groups of 2 give ten mostly-empty groups — nearly the original problem back. Groups of 10 put almost everybody in two bars and the shape disappears. Four to six groups is the usual answer, and the real reason is that you want enough bars to see a shape and few enough that each one holds several values.

6. Looking for a comparison, not two descriptions: Nebula’s visits were shorter — eight of their ten were under 34 seconds, while eight of Comet’s thirteen took 38 seconds or more. Lesson 12’s structure applies here exactly.

7. You lose the individual values — inside a group everybody looks the same, and you can no longer find the longest visit from the graph. It is worth it because the shape of the whole set is what you are trying to see, and the shape was invisible before. The strongest answers name both halves of the trade.

8. Yes. The groups hold counts, and Comet had more buddies to start with, so Comet’s bars start with an advantage. Either compare as fractions of each team’s total — 8 out of 13 against 8 out of 10 — or say in the sentence how many each team had. Lesson 12’s rule has not gone away just because the data is continuous.

Now your own times

Your groups are already written on your sheet. Choosing them is the hardest part and it is not today’s job for you. Tallying and describing is.

Add up your group counts. Do they come to 10? If not, one value has been put in two groups or missed.

Title it and label both axes. Then describe the shape in one sentence and say what the grouping has hidden.

What grouping buys and what it costs

“Before we grouped, we had thirteen numbers and no shape. Now we have five groups and a shape, and we can compare two teams at a glance.

But look at what happened to Oliver’s 38 and somebody else’s 41. In the graph they are now exactly the same thing. We cannot see the longest visit any more. We traded the detail for the shape, and that was a good trade — but it was a trade, and you should be able to say so.”

A set of intervals works when…It is broken when…
Every group is the same width.One group is wider, so it collects more values for free.
Every value falls in exactly one group.Boundaries overlap, like 30–35 and 35–40.
There are no gaps between the groups.A value lands between two groups and gets lost.
The counts add to the number of values you have.They do not. That is how you find the other three faults.
The check that catches everything: add your group counts. If the total is not the number of measurements you started with, something is overlapping, missing or miscounted.

Column graph, no gaps

Grouped continuous data is drawn with the bars touching, because the groups run into each other along a scale — there is no space between “34 to 37” and “38 to 41” where a value could live. Categorical bars are drawn apart, because there genuinely is nothing between Gold and Silver. The chart in the workbook is set that way already; it is worth a sentence, not a lesson.

Before Lesson 14

Keep one grouped display from each team. Next lesson opens with a newsletter article about Games Day that is misleading in four different ways, and it lands far harder when the class has their own honest displays to hold it against.

Exit ticket

On your own. Five minutes.
1SF

Twelve measurements: 21, 24, 22, 29, 31, 26, 33, 25, 28, 30, 23, 27. Put them into the groups 21–25, 26–30, 31–35. Check your counts add to 12.

2CF

A team uses the groups 20–25, 25–30 and 30–35. Name the problem and write a set of groups that fixes it.

3CU

Someone says “grouping the data is cheating, because you are hiding the real numbers”. Write a reply.

The answers

1. 21–25: five (21, 22, 23, 24, 25). 26–30: five (26, 27, 28, 29, 30). 31–35: two (31, 33). Total 12. A student whose total is not 12 has a boundary problem, and that is the diagnosis to make.

2. 25 and 30 are each in two groups. Any fix that is equal width with no overlap: 20–24, 25–29, 30–34.

3. The answer the lesson is for: it is true that detail is hidden, and that is the trade, not the cheat. The cheat would be unequal groups or boundaries chosen to make one bar look tall. A strong response says both — grouping does hide something, and it is honest as long as the groups are equal and the data is not being steered. That sentence is exactly where Lesson 14 begins.

Where this sits in the achievement standard

AC9M6ST01 names continuous numerical variables explicitly, and grouping is what makes them displayable at this level. This lesson completes the four data types begun in Lesson 9.

In the folio it produces item B4 — group your continuous data into intervals, build a display, and describe its shape. The interval choice has to be sensible and the description has to be of the grouped shape, not of individual bars.

The two ends of the room

A student who cannot yet tally reliably into supplied groups is working at the Year 5 standard — interpreting and comparing displays — and supplied intervals with a worked example is the right adjustment. A student asking what happens between the groups, or wanting to draw the bars at different widths, is reaching for the histogram, which is Year 7 and beyond. Name it, show them one, and leave it there.
