Lesson 12 · Whose game did they like more?

Two distributions, side by side. The skill is naming a feature that differs rather than describing each one in turn, and the trap is comparing raw counts when the two groups are not the same size. Both of those are in the achievement standard and both are in the folio.

This lesson does not depend on anybody’s partner being present. Comet and Nebula are supplied and they carry the whole taught section. A student whose partner team is away, or whose own data is thin, compares their data with Nebula’s and loses nothing. The withdrawal group can run the entire lesson on the two supplied sets.

Describing twice is not comparing

“Comet’s mode was 3. Nebula’s mode was 2.” Two facts, no comparison. The sentence the standard is asking for names the feature that differs and says what it means: Comet’s ratings sat higher than Nebula’s — most of Comet’s buddies gave a 3 or 4, while Nebula’s spread right across the scale and included the only 1.

Give them the structure once and then expect it: name the feature, give both values, say what it means.

The counts trap is the mathematical heart of the lesson

Comet had 13 buddies with a rating and Nebula had 10. Eleven of Comet’s gave a 3 or 4; five of Nebula’s did. Eleven against five looks like a landslide. As fractions it is 85% against 50% — still a clear difference, but a different-sized one, and the honest one.

This is the same move as Part A. There, the gap between observed and expected had to be read as a fraction of the trials rather than as a raw difference. Here, a count has to be read as a fraction of the buddies. Say that out loud: it is the one idea this unit returns to most.

Differentiation

Support

  • Counts supplied for both teams in one table — no spreadsheet work needed.
  • Three questions instead of eight.
  • A comparison frame: “______ was higher than ______, because ______.”
  • Fractions given alongside the counts, so the comparison is the task and the division is not.

Core

  • All eight questions.
  • Works out the fractions, with a calculator.
  • Compares own data with the partner team’s.

Extension

  • Two prompts, the first of which asks them to build a misleading display on purpose.
  • That is Lesson 14 in miniature. Students who do it today lead the critique groups on the day.
Lesson-specific adjustment today: the supplied fractions, so that a student who cannot yet divide reliably is still doing the comparing, which is what is being assessed.

What we are learning today

We are learning to compare two sets of data by naming a feature that is different between them, and to be careful about comparing counts when the two groups are not the same size.

I will know I can do it when…

  • I can write a sentence that compares, rather than two sentences that describe;
  • I can say when a raw count is a fair comparison and when it is not;
  • I can turn a count into a fraction of the group it came from;
  • I can choose a display that lets somebody see the difference at a glance.

Set your goal for today

Read the success criteria above. Which one is the biggest stretch for you? Write one thing you are going to focus on.

Not sure? The first one. Almost everybody writes two descriptions the first time and thinks they have compared.

How hard is each question?

Every question in this unit is labelled so you know what kind of thinking it needs.

SF — simple familiar

Read a mode or a total off a table you have been given.

CF — complex familiar

Turn counts into fractions, then say which team’s buddies enjoyed it more and why.

CU — complex unfamiliar

Decide what a difference actually proves, and what you would still need to know.

A comparison names one feature. Mode, spread, shape, or the fraction at the top end — pick the one that differs most, and say what it means. Listing everything about both teams is not a comparison, it is two descriptions with a full stop between them.

Warm-up · is that a description?

Five minutes. Read each one out. Thumbs up if it describes the data, thumbs down if it describes the picture.

1. “The tallest bar is in the middle.” — Picture.

2. “Most buddies won one turn out of three.” — Data.

3. “There is nothing above the 1.” — Picture. Rewrite it: nobody gave it a 1.

4. “Only one buddy out of fourteen won all three turns.” — Data, and a good one — it says how many out of how many.

Point at number 4 before you move on

“One out of fourteen” is the habit today’s lesson is built on. A count carried with the size of its group is a statement somebody can check and compare. A count on its own is not. Students who picked that habit up in Lesson 11 will find the fractions work today easy; the ones who did not are about to find out why it mattered.

“Here are two sentences. One of them compares and one of them does not.

A. Comet’s mode was 3. Nebula’s mode was 2.

B. Comet’s ratings sat higher than Nebula’s — most of Comet’s buddies gave a 3 or a 4, while Nebula’s spread right across the scale and included the only 1 anybody gave.

A is two facts with a full stop between them. B names the thing that is different and says what it means. B is what I am asking for today.”

The structure: name the feature → give both values → say what it means.

Then set the trap, and let them fall into it

Ask: which team’s buddies enjoyed it more? Someone will say Comet, because 11 gave a 3 or 4 against Nebula’s 5. Do not correct it. Ask how many buddies each team had. Then let the room work out that it is 11 out of 13 against 5 out of 10 — 85% against 50%.

The conclusion does not change here, which is exactly what makes it a safe place to meet the idea. The difference is real; it is just not as big as 11 against 5 made it look. A trap where the answer flips would teach them to distrust the method rather than to use it.

Comparing Comet and Nebula

Work from resources/l12-03-comparing-two-teams.xlsx, sheet Side by side, or from the table on your worksheet. A calculator is allowed from question 4 on.
1SF

What is the mode of Nebula’s ratings? What is the mode of Comet’s?

2SF

How many buddies gave a rating for each team?

3CF

Someone says “Comet got five 4s and Nebula only got two, so Comet’s game was more than twice as good”. What is wrong with that reasoning?

4CF

Work out the fraction of each team’s buddies who gave a 3 or a 4. Write each one as a fraction and as a percentage.

5CF

Write one sentence comparing the two sets of ratings. Name the feature that differs — do not describe each team in turn.

6CF

Two separate column graphs, or one graph with both teams side by side? Which lets somebody see the difference at a glance, and why?

7CU

Six of Nebula’s ten buddies won nothing at all. Four of Comet’s fourteen won nothing. Does that mean Nebula’s game is unfair? What else would you need to know before you could say?

8CU

The two teams had different numbers of buddies. Name one comparison you can still make safely using raw counts, and one you cannot.

1. Nebula 2, Comet 3.

2. Comet 13, Nebula 10. Both teams had more buddies than that — Comet had 15 and Nebula 10 — but two of Comet’s left before they were asked. Students who answer 15 have not noticed the blanks, which is Lesson 10 arriving back.

3. Two faults, and the better answers get both. The counts come from groups of different sizes, so 5 against 2 is not a fair comparison. And “more than twice as good” treats the number of 4s as a measure of quality, when a rating scale does not work like that — two 4s and three 3s is not obviously worse than five 4s and nothing else.

4. Comet 11/13, which is about 85%. Nebula 5/10, which is 50%. Accept 0.85 and 0.5. A student who writes 11/13 = 11% has divided the wrong way round; send them to the conversion mat.

5. Looking for the structure: a named feature, both values, and what it means. Comet’s ratings sat higher — 85% of their buddies gave a 3 or 4, against half of Nebula’s — which suggests Treasure Bag went down better with the Preps. Two separate descriptions joined by “and” is not it.

6. One graph with both teams side by side, because the eye compares heights that are next to each other. With two separate graphs the axes can be different without anybody noticing, which is exactly the trick Lesson 14 is about.

7. The payoff of the whole unit. No. Hard is not the same as unfair. Nebula’s Lucky Dip has one gold token in a bag of eight, so each turn has a probability of 1/8, and over three turns the chance of winning at least once is about 33%. They observed 40%, which is close. The game is behaving exactly as its probabilities say it should — it is a fair game that is hard to win. To answer this you need the sample space and the probabilities, not the results. That is Part A, back again.

8. Safe: the mode, and the shape — neither depends on how many buddies there were. Also safe is anything about one team on its own. Not safe: any sentence of the form “more buddies did X for this team” used as evidence that the team’s game was better, because a bigger group produces bigger counts for free.

One sentence that compares

“Everybody read me your comparison sentence. I am listening for three things: the feature, both numbers, and what it means.

If your sentence has a full stop in the middle and the second half starts with the other team’s name, you have described twice. That is not wrong — it is just not yet a comparison.”

Safe to compare with raw countsNot safe
The mode. It does not depend on how many buddies there were.“More buddies gave us a 4” as evidence that your game was better.
The shape — bunched, spread out, one big group.Any total, when the two groups are different sizes.
Anything about one team on its own.“Twice as many”, unless the groups are the same size.
The move that fixes almost all of it: carry the group size with the count. Not “eleven buddies”, but “eleven out of thirteen”. Say it that way every time and the unfair comparison becomes very hard to make by accident.

The thread back to Part A

Worth making explicit before they leave: in Part A they learned that the gap between observed and expected has to be read as a fraction of the number of trials, not as a raw difference. Today they learned that a count has to be read as a fraction of the group it came from. It is the same idea twice, in the two halves of the folio, and students who see that connection write much better Part B answers.

Tomorrow’s problem, flagged today

Someone will ask why the visit times are not in any of this. Good. The answer is that every time is different, so a column graph of times would have one bar per buddy and show nothing. That is Lesson 13, and the question is better asked by a student than announced by you.

Exit ticket

On your own. Five minutes.
1SF

Team Orion: 9 of 12 buddies won at least one turn. Team Vega: 7 of 10. Write each as a percentage.

2CF

Write one sentence comparing Orion and Vega. Name the feature, give both values, say what it means.

3CU

A third team says “20 of our buddies won at least one turn, which is more than both of you, so our game is the easiest to win”. What would you ask them before you agreed?

The answers

1. Orion 9/12 = 75%. Vega 7/10 = 70%. Both friendly on a calculator, and the two are close on purpose.

2. The comparison the numbers support is a small one: Orion’s buddies won slightly more often — 75% against 70% — but the two games are very close. A student who writes “Orion is much better because 9 is more than 7” has made both of today’s errors in one sentence, and that is the response worth following up.

3. How many buddies did you have? If the answer is 40, then 20 out of 40 is 50%, which is the lowest of the three. The question to ask is the group size, every time.

Where this sits in the achievement standard

AC9M6ST01 asks students to compare data sets using comparative displays. The comparing is the verb being assessed, and a response that describes two displays separately has not met it.

In the folio this is item B5 — compare your two distributions, name the feature that differs, and say what it tells you. The fraction-of-the-group habit also carries item B6, because the misleading article in Lesson 14 uses a count from a tiny sample as though it were a rate.

For the students who are ahead

Comparing the middle of two distributions is Year 7 territory, where the mean and the median arrive. If a student reaches for an average, let them, and name what they are doing. Do not make it the class task — at Year 6 the comparison is built from mode, spread, shape and proportion, and a student who can do those four well is at the standard.
