What has to be on the desk before they start

Their own cleaned file, their decision log from Lesson 10, their displays from Lessons 11 and 13, and a clean copy of the newsletter article. A student missing the decision log cannot answer B2 from memory three weeks later, and B2 is Reasoning evidence.

B6 is the item that carries the grade

With A7 it is one of the two complex unfamiliar items in the whole folio. A student can be accurate on every other item and still sit at C. That is deliberate — the Year 6 achievement standard is the C standard, and the discriminating evidence has to come from somewhere the task does not scaffold.

So do not prompt on B6. No protocol cards, no “have you looked at the axis”, no list of the five faults on the board. The lesson taught them how; today shows whether they can.

Two students from one team share data and should not share sentences

They have the same rows, the same displays and the same decision log. B2, B5 and B6 are theirs alone. Identical wording between teammates is worth investigating before any judgement is recorded.

What today is for

Today you show what you can do on your own with the data your team collected, and with an article that is true and misleading at the same time.

I will show that I can…

  • state a statistical question and name the type of each variable I collected;
  • describe how we collected the data and name one real limitation of the way we did it;
  • build a display for a categorical variable and describe it by mode and shape;
  • group my continuous data into intervals and describe the grouped shape;
  • compare two distributions by naming the feature that differs;
  • critique the newsletter article and explain the effect each fault has on a reader;
  • redraw one of its graphs fairly and say what changed.

Set your goal for today

Read the list above. Which one is the biggest stretch for you today? Write it down before you start.

Not sure? B6 is the longest piece of writing in the folio. Leave yourself fifteen minutes for it, not five.

How hard is each question?

Every item today is labelled, the same as in every lesson of this unit.

SF — simple familiar

B1 and B3. You have done both of these before with this same data.

CF — complex familiar

B2, B4, B5 and B7. More than one step, and you choose the method.

CU — complex unfamiliar

B6, on its own. Nobody tells you what is wrong with the article or how many things to find.

B6 asks for the effect on a reader, not a list. Naming a fault is the start of the answer. Saying what it makes somebody believe, and why that is not what the numbers show, is the answer.

Before you start — four checks

Five minutes, on your own, with everything in front of you. Not marked.

1. Can you open your team’s cleaned file right now? Open it before we start, not during.

2. Do you have your decision log from Lesson 10? B2 asks what was wrong with your data and what you did about it. The log is that answer.

3. Do you have your displays from Lessons 11 and 13, or can you rebuild them? You are not starting from the paper sheets today.

4. Do you have a clean copy of the newsletter article, with nothing written on it?

Fix a missing file now, not at minute thirty

A student without their cleaned file can use their teammate’s copy — the data is shared, the writing is not. A student without a decision log answers B2 from what they remember and says so, which is an honest answer and is worth more than a reconstructed one.

A student without the article gets a fresh copy. There is no reason to make that cost them anything.

The conditions, read out

“This is Part B. It joins the Part A you did at the end of last term, and I mark the two together as one folio.

You work on your own. You can use your own cleaned file, your decision log, your displays, the newsletter article, a calculator and the conversion mat. You cannot use the Lesson 14 worksheet or the protocol cards, and you cannot talk to your team.

There are seven items. B1 and B3 you have done before with this data. B4 and B5 are the grouping and the comparing. B6 is the article, and it is the longest piece of writing in the whole folio — leave yourself fifteen minutes for it. B7 is redrawing one graph properly.

If your file will not open, put your hand up now rather than sitting there.”

No protocol cards today, and no list of faults on the board. B6 is where the A and B evidence lives, and a prompt that names a fault hands over exactly what is being judged. If a student is stuck, the only allowable prompt is to read the article again.

Part B · seven items

This is the assessment

Your teacher will enter the release code to show the items. Everything you write goes in your paper booklet.

Work on your own, in order. Use your own cleaned file, your decision log, your displays and the article. Write everything in your booklet.

B1SF

State your statistical question. Then name every variable your team collected and say what type each one is.

B2CF

Describe how your data was collected. Then identify one limitation of the way you did it, and say what difference that limitation could make to your results.

B3SF

Build a display for one categorical variable. Describe it by its mode and its shape.

B4CF

Group your continuous data into intervals, build a display, and describe the shape of the grouped data.

B5CF

Compare two distributions — your team and your partner team, or two variables of your own. Name the feature that differs and say what it tells you.

B6CU

Critique the newsletter article. Identify what is misleading about it and explain the effect each fault would have on somebody reading it.

B7CF

Redraw one graph from the article fairly. Explain what changed, and what did not change.

B1 wants a question with a group, a variable and a way of measuring it — not a yes or no question about one person. The four types must be correct; the usual error is calling the visit times discrete because they were written as whole seconds.

B2 is answered from the decision log, not from memory. A real limitation is one they met: the enjoyment scale had to be the same for every team so a six-point idea was dropped; times were written down at the end rather than between buddies; two buddies walked off; the outcome was recorded for the first turn only. The second half of the item is what separates C from B — what difference could it make. “We might have made mistakes” is not a limitation of a method.

B3 and B4 are the Fluency items: an appropriate display for the data type, sensible equal intervals that account for every value, and a description of the data rather than the bars. A grouped display whose counts do not add to the number of measurements has an overlap or a gap in it.

B5 must name a feature. Two descriptions joined by “and” is the common C-level response. Where the two groups are different sizes, a comparison of raw counts without any mention of the group sizes is a Reasoning error, not a slip.

B6. The item that carries the folio. Five faults are available: the truncated axis and the claim drawn from it; four buddies turned into a percentage of all Preps; “twice as many” with no starting number; one stall standing for every stall; and the conclusion about learning, which nothing in the article measures. Finding faults is not the grade — explaining the effect on a reader is. A response that lists all five without explaining any sits lower than one that takes two apart properly. The last fault is the one the strongest students find, and a student who reaches it has done work beyond the Year 6 standard.

B7 needs both halves. What changed: with the axis at zero the three bars are nearly the same height and the “three times” claim collapses. What did not change: the numbers, and the fact that Comet was still the most popular. A student who writes only the first half has fixed the graph without understanding why it was wrong.

While you work

These change how you record, not what you are asked. The items are identical. Using one of these is not worth fewer marks.
  • The booklet with extra recording structure — same seven items, more room and sentence starters.
  • The conversion mat on your desk from the start. You do not have to ask.
  • The intervals given to you for B4, if that is how you worked in Lesson 13.
  • Someone to write for you on B2 and B6 if that is in your plan. You say it, they write it.
  • Extra time if that is in your plan.

If you are not sure whether something is allowed, put your hand up and ask. Asking is never a problem.

Work in order, B1 to B7. B1 and B3 are quick, because you have done both with this data before — do not spend long on them. B4 before B5, so you have both displays in front of you when you compare. Then stop and look at the clock: B6 needs about fifteen minutes and it is the longest thing you will write in the whole folio.

Stuck on B6? Read the article again, slowly, one sentence at a time. Remember that every sentence in it is true — so do not look for a lie, look for what it makes you believe.

These are not marked and are not part of your folio. They are here so that finishing early is still thinking time. Do them in the back of your booklet.
★1CU

Write the headline and the first paragraph of an honest article about Games Day that still reads as good news. What did you have to give up?

★2CU

If you ran Games Day again next year, name one thing you would change about how the data is collected, and say exactly what it would let you find out that you cannot find out now.

Collecting the folio

“Pens down. Staple Part B on top of Part A — Part A behind, Part B in front, decision log at the back. Name on all three.

Before you hand it up, read your B6 answer one more time. For each fault you named, have you said what it would make somebody believe? If you have only said what is wrong, you have given me half an answer. Two minutes.”

No discussion of the article today. The conversation about what people found happens after the folios are in, and it is worth having — but not before the work is collected.

The two-minute B6 check

As in Part A, this is the only prompt given during the assessment, it goes to everybody at once, and it names a requirement that is already written in the item. It lifts a handful of students from a list to an explanation without telling anybody what to find.

Judging the folio

Four proficiencies, judged separately across both parts, then an on-balance judgement. Start with A7 and B6 — those two carry it, and the rest of the folio confirms what they tell you. A folio that is accurate throughout and explains nothing is a C, not an A: accuracy is Fluency, and it does not substitute for Reasoning.

Where a Personalised Learning Plan specifies adjusted criteria for the written justifications, record the judgement against the adjusted criteria and note it on the folio.

Moderation

Moderate with the Year 6 team before reporting, using A7 and B6 first. Bring two folios you are confident about and one you are not.

Question 4 is the one that matters

If a reasonable number of the class say “where the axis starts” or “how many people they asked”, the unit has done what it set out to do. That is the habit AC9M6ST02 is actually asking for, and it is the only part of this unit they will still be using in ten years.

Where Part B sits in the achievement standard

B1 and B2 evidence AC9M6ST03 — planning and conducting an investigation, including acquiring and validating the data. B3, B4 and B5 evidence AC9M6ST01 across all four variable types, with the comparison as the verb that matters. B6 and B7 evidence AC9M6ST02, interpreting and critiquing a statistically informed argument.

Taken with Part A, the folio covers all five extracts of the achievement standard this unit set out to address. It is reported as one result, not two.

What to keep for next year

The collection sheets, a photograph of the display board, two or three folios across the range with the students’ names removed, and your own note on which teams’ games produced usable data and which did not. That last one is worth more than anything else here: the games that fail are always the ones with no sample space, and knowing which they were makes the Lesson 7 brief sharper next time.
